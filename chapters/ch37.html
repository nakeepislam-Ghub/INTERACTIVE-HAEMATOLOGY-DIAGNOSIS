37.1Lymphocytes

  • Origin: haemopoietic stem cell → common lymphoid progenitor → B, T and NK cells.
  • Adaptive immunity: antibodies (B cells), cytotoxic granules (cytotoxic T cells) and signals to other immune cells (helper T cells).
  • Innate immunity: NK cells, CD3+CD56+ NK-like T cells and γδ T cells.
  • Lifespan: weeks to several years.

37.2B lymphocytes

A mature B cell is defined by surface immunoglobulin: two heavy and two light chains, each with constant and variable regions.

  • Heavy chain (chromosome 14) sets the class: µ (IgM), δ (IgD), γ (IgG), α (IgA), ε (IgE).
  • Light chain: κ (chromosome 2) or λ (chromosome 22). Each B cell uses one light chain for life. Normal B cells are a mix, with a κ:λ ratio of about 3:1; a strongly skewed ratio points to a clone.
  • B-cell receptor: membrane Ig with CD79a (Igα) and CD79b (Igβ), which carry the signal.

Antigen-independent stage (bone marrow)

  • Heavy chain rearrangement: ordered joining of variable (V), diversity (D) and joining (J) segments that encode the antigen-binding part.
  • Light chain rearrangement: the same, but light chains have no D segment.
  • Pre-B cells with cytoplasmic µ, then naive B cells (surface IgM+IgD+, CD5+) that recirculate and fill primary follicles and mantle zones. Self-reactive clones are eliminated in the marrow.

Antigen-dependent stage (lymph node and spleen)

  • Naive B cells meet antigen. In the spleen they become follicular or marginal zone B cells; some form a germinal centre.
  • Somatic hypermutation diversifies the variable regions; class switch changes the heavy-chain constant region according to local cytokines.
  • Centroblasts mature into centrocytes in the light zone, which become memory B cells (pan-B antigens, IgM with low IgD, CD5− CD10−, in blood, marginal zones and MALT) or plasma cells (IRF4, CD79a, CD38, CD138, IgG or IgA).
Germinal centre B cells express low surface Ig, low BCL2, CD10, LMO2 (specific for germinal centre cells in reactive tissue, though also found in marrow precursors), HGAL (GCET2) and BCL6. BCL6 and IRF4 are reciprocal: IRF4 switches off BCL6 in centrocytes and plasma cells after CD40 ligand and CD23 signals from T and follicular dendritic cells. MYC is needed to start the germinal centre and is re-induced in light-zone centrocytes so they can re-enter the dark zone.

37.3T lymphocytes

T cells arise in the marrow and mature in the thymus. Cortical thymocytes express TdT, CD1a, CD3 (first in the cytoplasm), CD5 and CD7, and start CD4−CD8−, then become CD4+CD8+. Medullary thymocytes look like mature T cells and express either CD4 or CD8.

37.4Natural killer cells

  • Arise in the marrow. The best way to tell them from T cells is the absence of a TCR and of surface CD3; activated NK cells have cytoplasmic CD3 ε and ζ chains.
  • Markers: CD2, CD7, CD16, CD56, sometimes CD8 and CD57.
  • They kill by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity or via activating and inhibitory killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs). Antibodies to KIRs are used to test clonality in NK proliferations.
  • Toll-like receptors recognise microbes and signal through NF-κB.

37.7Infectious mononucleosis

Definition: lymphocytes 50% or more of white cells, with over 10% reactive lymphocytes, in response to infection.

TypeMain featuresCauses
PharyngealSore throat, lethargyEBV
GlandularLymphadenopathyCMV, HIV, HHV-6, metapneumovirus, rubella, hepatitis A, adenovirus, Toxoplasma gondii, Bartonella henselae, Brucella abortus
TyphoidalFever, diarrhoeaCMV

How EBV causes it

  • Spread by saliva and sexual contact (CMV also spreads congenitally, in breast milk, by contact and by transfusion; it infects monocytes and macrophages).
  • Targets: B cells, and oropharyngeal epithelium, where the virus replicates although it lacks CD21 and HLA class II.
  • Entry into B cells needs viral gH, gL and gp42, CD21 (the C3d receptor) and HLA class II as the co-receptor for gp42.
  • Virus released from epithelium infects B cells in Waldeyer's ring; they shed CD23 and make an autocrine growth factor.
  • Infected B cells stay latent (expressing EBNA-1, 2, 3A, 3B, 3C, EBNA-LP and LMP-1, 2A, 2B), spread through the body as a reservoir, or reactivate, lyse and shed virus into saliva. After recovery there is lifelong low-level infection.
  • Only 1 in 105–106 B cells is infected, yet 1–5% of cytotoxic T cells respond: these are the reactive lymphocytes. If this T-cell control fails, an aggressive T-cell and histiocytic reaction with haemophagocytosis follows, and can be rapidly fatal (Chapter 38).

Clinical picture

The triad is fever, pharyngitis and generalised lymphadenopathy. Incubation is up to 7 weeks; the illness lasts about 3 weeks.

  • Sore throat, anorexia, nausea, palatal petechiae at the junction of hard and soft palate.
  • Bilateral non-tender cervical nodes, plus axillary and inguinal nodes.
  • Splenomegaly in the first week, settling after the second; slight hepatomegaly and jaundice in 10%.
  • Macular rash, especially after ampicillin.

Complications: Burkitt lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma; other lymphoproliferative disease and some solid tumours; autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, immune thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, marrow aplasia, haemophagocytic syndrome; acquired immune deficiency; chronic active infection; splenic rupture; hepatitis, pneumonia, airway obstruction, myocarditis, encephalitis, Guillain–Barré syndrome. In pregnancy: abortion and congenital anomalies.

  • X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome (Duncan syndrome): a specific immune defect against EBV, ending in death or B-cell lymphoma.
  • Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder: under immunosuppression, EBV-infected B cells grow, from polyclonal hyperplasia to monoclonal lymphoma.

Full recovery is usual within 2 months.

Laboratory findings

  • White count: leucopenia in week 1, then leucocytosis (10–20 ×10⁹/L, sometimes more) with absolute lymphocytosis in weeks 2–3, lasting 1–2 months.
  • Reactive lymphocytes over 10%, mostly CD8+ T cells, some CD4+ T cells and NK cells.

  • Heterophile antibodies: Paul–Bunnell (patient serum agglutinates sheep red cells, titre over 1/112; low titres occur in healthy people, some leukaemias and lymphomas and serum sickness). Monospot (formalinised horse red cells) is often negative in children and adults over 25.

  • Marrow only to exclude leukaemia or lymphoma; a node biopsy can be hard to tell from lymphoma.
Differential diagnosis: acute pharyngitis with reactive lymphocytosis must be separated from leukaemia (immunophenotyping) and lymphoma (biopsy).

37.8HIV and the blood

  • Cytopenias from HIV itself or opportunistic infection (infected progenitors; more importantly infected stromal and accessory cells with altered cytokines; anti-HIV antibodies against infected cells), from lymphoma, and from drugs.
  • Lupus anticoagulant. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Low CD4+ count and an inverted CD4:CD8 ratio.

The marrow in HIV

  • Dysplasia, increasing with disease stage. Erythroid dysplasia in over half: megaloblastic change unrelated to B12, folate or drugs (though drugs add to it), bi- or multinucleated erythroblasts, irregular nuclei, basophilic stippling, ring sideroblasts. Megakaryocytic dysplasia in over a third: hyperplasia, micromegakaryocytes, hypolobulation, bare nuclei, clusters. Granulocytic: giant metamyelocytes, nuclear fragments, Pelger cells, abnormally placed immature precursors, apoptosis.
  • Cellularity: increased in 50%, decreased in 15%. Pure red cell hypoplasia suggests parvovirus B19.
  • Fibrosis in 20–50%; reticuloendothelial iron block; more histiocytes, with haemophagocytosis in advanced immunosuppression.
  • Plasma cells increased, sometimes atypical or clustered; polyclonal gammopathy with or without a paraprotein.
  • Opportunistic organisms (acid-fast bacilli, leishmania, histoplasma, pneumocystis, cryptococcus), granulomas, reactive lymphoid nodules; AIDS-related lymphoma involves the marrow in 20%.

37.9Autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS)

A non-malignant lymphoproliferation with autoimmune cytopenias and a higher risk of lymphoma.

  • Cause: an autosomal dominant germline (or somatic) mutation of FAS, less often of other apoptosis-signalling proteins.
  • Pathogenesis: the apoptosis that normally removes activated lymphocytes after an infection fails, so lymphocytes accumulate and secondary cancers arise.

Clinical picture: most often at about 2 years of age, marked in infancy and regressing in adolescence. Lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly (75%), no systemic symptoms. Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, ITP or immune neutropenia in a third. Rash and urticaria are common. Lymphoma in about 10%. Rarely Guillain–Barré syndrome, optic neuritis, primary biliary cirrhosis, autoimmune hepatitis, glomerulonephritis, arthritis, vasculitis, acquired haemophilia or IgA deficiency.

Laboratory: absolute lymphocytosis; marked increase in double-negative T cells (thought to be cytotoxic T cells that lost CD8); defective FAS-mediated apoptosis in vitro (T cells cultured for over 10 days with anti-FAS IgM do not die); high serum FAS ligand, IL-10, IL-18 and vitamin B12; FAS gene mutation; polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia; Coombs test and autoantibodies (anticardiolipin, ANA, rheumatoid factor) may be positive.

Diagnosis: definitive = required criteria 1 and 2 plus one more required criterion. Probable = required criteria 1 and 2 plus one supporting criterion. Check your patient with the ALPS criteria tool.

Differential diagnosis: viral infection, autoimmune disease, acute leukaemia, lymphoma, IgA deficiency, common variable immune deficiency.
