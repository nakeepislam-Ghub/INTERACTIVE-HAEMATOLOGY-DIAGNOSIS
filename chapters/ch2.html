2.1Body iron and its jobs

Total body iron is 3–5 g (men 50 mg/kg, women 40 mg/kg).

Besides oxygen transport, iron runs cytochromes and the electron transport chain, cyclooxygenase, catalase, ribonucleotide reductase (DNA synthesis), myeloperoxidase and T-cell function, and keeps epithelia and nervous tissue healthy.

2.2Intake, absorption and transport

  • Diet: 10–20 mg a day, of which only 1–2 mg (5–10%) reaches the circulation. Rich sources are liver, red meat, egg yolk and dried beans; milk is poor.
  • Non-haem iron (duodenum, upper jejunum): Fe³⁺ is reduced by duodenal cytochrome b, enters through DMT1, is stored as ferritin or leaves through ferroportin, is reoxidised by hephaestin and binds transferrin.
  • Haem iron enters through haem carrier protein 1 and is freed by haem oxygenase; it is absorbed better and is not affected by luminal factors.
  • Absorption rises with acid, vitamin C, sugars and amino acids, iron deficiency (up to 30%), anaemia, hypoxia and active or ineffective erythropoiesis. It falls with tannins, phytates, phosphates and oxalates, alkaline pH (gastrectomy, achlorhydria), full stores (2–5%) and inflammation.
  • Transferrin carries 2 Fe³⁺ atoms per molecule to cells with transferrin receptor 1 (CD71), most dense on intermediate normoblasts. The complex is endocytosed, acidified, iron is released and the receptor recycles.

About 20–25 mg of iron a day is needed for red cells, and 95% of it comes from recycling old red cells in macrophages. Daily intake equals daily loss (1–2 mg) through shed gut cells, urine, skin and menstruation.

2.3Regulation: IRP/IRE and hepcidin

  • Cellular iron is controlled by iron regulatory proteins (IRP1, IRP2) binding iron-responsive elements on mRNA. When iron is low, IRPs bind: at the 3′ end (TfR1, DMT1) this stabilises mRNA and increases protein; at the 5′ end (ferritin, ALAS2) it blocks translation.
  • Systemic iron is controlled by hepcidin (HAMP, chromosome 19), mainly from the liver. It binds ferroportin and causes its internalisation and degradation, so iron is trapped in gut cells, macrophages and hepatocytes.
  • Hepcidin rises with high iron stores and inflammation (IL-6). It falls with iron deficiency, anaemia, hypoxia, active or ineffective erythropoiesis and in primary haemochromatosis.

2.4Iron investigations

What it measuresTests
StoresSerum ferritin; Perls stain of marrow, liver or skin; liver iron by biopsy, MRI or SQUID; desferrioxamine chelation test; phlebotomy test
Supply to tissuesSerum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation, soluble transferrin receptor, red cell protoporphyrin, % hypochromic red cells, reticulocyte Hb content
FunctionHb, MCV, MCH
  • Ferritin (men 15–300, women 15–200 µg/L) tracks stores. It is low only in iron deficiency, but rises with inflammation, infection, malignancy, liver necrosis and non-use of iron (aplastic, megaloblastic).
  • Serum iron (60–170 µg/dL) and TIBC (250–400 µg/dL) vary from day to day. TIBC is high only in iron deficiency.
  • Transferrin saturation (15–50%) is the earliest test to change in overload; below 15% cannot support erythropoiesis.
  • Soluble transferrin receptor is high in iron deficiency but normal in anaemia of chronic disorders; the sTfR ÷ log ferritin ratio separates them better.
  • Hypochromic red cells over 6% and reticulocyte Hb under 32 pg are early signs of iron-deficient erythropoiesis.

Perls (Prussian blue) stain of the marrow

  • Normal: 30–40% of normoblasts are sideroblasts with a few scattered granules; iron in macrophages.
  • Iron deficiency: sideroblasts and macrophage iron both absent or reduced.
  • Anaemia of chronic disorders: few sideroblasts but plenty of macrophage iron.
  • Iron overload: more sideroblasts and coarse granules everywhere.
  • Ring sideroblasts: at least 6 granules around the nucleus, or covering a third of it. Seen in sideroblastic anaemia, myelofibrosis, AML (especially erythroid and therapy-related) and AML with dysplasia.

2.5Iron deficiency anaemia

The commonest anaemia.

  • Causes: chronic blood loss of at least 6–8 mL a day (commonest: menstrual, GI, urinary); higher needs (prematurity, infancy, adolescence, pregnancy, lactation); poor absorption (malabsorption, gastrectomy, achlorhydria); poor intake.
  1. Store depletion (prelatent): ferritin and marrow haemosiderin fall.
  2. Iron-deficient erythropoiesis (latent): low serum iron, high TIBC, saturation under 15%, high sTfR and protoporphyrin, MCV and MCH start to fall.
  3. Iron deficiency anaemia: Hb and PCV fall, saturation under 10%.
  4. Ineffective erythropoiesis with mild haemolysis.
  5. Tissue effects: koilonychia, angular stomatitis, glossitis, pharyngeal web (Plummer–Vinson, Paterson–Kelly), pica, poor immunity and work capacity, delayed development in infants, premature labour.
  • Blood: low Hb, MCV, MCH, MCHC, high RDW, often thrombocytosis. Film: microcytic hypochromic cells, pencil cells (elliptocytes), target cells.
  • Marrow: erythroid hyperplasia with small, ragged late normoblasts; no stainable iron.
  • Earliest changes: RDW up, ferritin down, protoporphyrin and sTfR up, more hypochromic cells.
  • Always find the cause: occult blood, stool, endoscopy, urine, pelvic examination.
  • Response to iron: reticulocytes rise from day 4 and peak at 1–2 weeks; Hb rises about 2 g/dL every 3 weeks.

2.6Iron overload (haemochromatosis)

Iron builds up in parenchymal organs and damages them through lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation and lysosomal injury.

  • Causes: hereditary haemochromatosis; ineffective erythropoiesis (thalassaemia major and intermedia, CDA, sideroblastic anaemia); excess iron intake (Sub-Saharan dietary overload, parenteral iron); repeated transfusion; modest overload in chronic liver disease, porphyria cutanea tarda, atransferrinaemia and aceruloplasminaemia.

  • Clinical: arrhythmias and cardiomyopathy; hepatomegaly, cirrhosis and hepatoma (25%); diabetes and endocrine failure; grey-bronze skin ("bronze diabetes"); arthritis of the hand joints and chondrocalcinosis.
  • Diagnosis: transferrin saturation over 55% in men, 50% in women (earliest and most sensitive); high ferritin and iron, low TIBC; liver iron over 80 µmol/g dry weight; HFE genotyping confirms; screen relatives.
  • Treatment: regular phlebotomy to saturation under 50% and ferritin under 50 µg/L; chelation (desferrioxamine, deferiprone, deferasirox) when venesection is not possible.

2.7Sideroblastic anaemia

Refractory anaemias from a block in haem (protoporphyrin) synthesis, so iron piles up in the mitochondria around the nucleus.

  • Hereditary: X-linked ALAS2 mutations (commonest; males, may respond to pyridoxine); X-linked with ataxia (ABCB7); mitochondrial (Pearson syndrome: pancytopenia, vacuolated precursors, pancreatic failure; Kearns–Sayre); autosomal forms (thiamine-responsive DIDMOAD, erythropoietic protoporphyria, glutaredoxin 5).
  • Acquired primary: MDS with ring sideroblasts.
  • Acquired secondary: isoniazid and pyridoxine deficiency, alcohol, chloramphenicol, lead, copper deficiency, hypothermia, and some inflammatory and malignant diseases.
  • Laboratory: microcytic hypochromic with a dimorphic film; reticulocytes low for the anaemia; high ferritin, iron and saturation, low TIBC; marrow erythroid hyperplasia with vacuolated precursors and ring sideroblasts.
  • Dimorphic film also follows iron treatment of iron deficiency, transfusion of a microcytic patient, and combined iron and B12 deficiency.
  • Treatment: pyridoxine, folic acid, transfusion with chelation. Avoid splenectomy: it does not fix the anaemia and the thrombocytosis causes thrombosis.

2.8Lead poisoning and the porphyrias

Lead (painters of old buildings, battery workers, children eating paint flakes) blocks ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, globin synthesis and pyrimidine 5′-nucleotidase, so RNA collects as basophilic stippling. Expect a mild, slightly hypochromic haemolytic anaemia with stippling, siderotic granules and sometimes ring sideroblasts, plus abdominal pain, neuropathy and encephalopathy. Confirm with blood and urine lead, high red cell protoporphyrin and urinary ALA and coproporphyrin.
