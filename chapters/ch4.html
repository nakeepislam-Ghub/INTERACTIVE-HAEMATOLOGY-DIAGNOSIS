Haemolytic anaemia: introduction

What happens to destroyed red cells, how haemolytic anaemias are classified, and the three kinds of evidence that prove haemolysis.

4.1Definition

Haemolytic anaemia is premature destruction of red cells that exceeds the capacity of a normally working marrow. Red cells normally live about 120 days, and the marrow can raise production 6–8 times, so a mild haemolysis may cause no anaemia at all (compensated haemolysis).

4.2Fate of destroyed red cells

IntravascularExtravascular
  • Free Hb binds haptoglobin, which is used up; the complex is cleared by the liver.
  • Hb beyond haptoglobin's capacity is filtered: haemoglobinuria. Iron left in tubular cells is shed as haemosiderinuria.
  • Free haem binds haemopexin, then albumin (methaemalbumin).
  • LDH released from the cells rises sharply.
  • Macrophages of the spleen and liver engulf the cells.
  • Globin goes to amino acids; haem oxygenase splits haem into iron and protoporphyrin, which becomes unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin.
  • Bilirubin passes into bile, is reduced to urobilinogen, and appears as stercobilinogen in stool and a little urobilinogen in urine.

4.3Classification

By siteCauses
Extravascular (most)Membrane disorders (spherocytosis, elliptocytosis, stomatocytosis, pyropoikilocytosis, acanthocytosis); enzyme disorders (G6PD, pyruvate kinase); haemoglobin disorders (thalassaemia, haemoglobinopathies); liver disease, hypersplenism, infection, some chemicals and most warm autoimmune haemolysis
IntravascularMismatched transfusion; acute G6PD haemolysis; red cell fragmentation (MAHA, valves); some autoimmune and drug-induced haemolysis; malaria, babesia, clostridia; PNH; unstable haemoglobins
  • Inherited causes are all corpuscular: membrane, enzyme and haemoglobin defects.
  • Acquired causes are immune (autoimmune, alloimmune, drug-induced) or non-immune (infection, chemical and physical agents, fragmentation, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, acquired membrane disorders).
  • PNH is the exception: acquired, yet intracorpuscular (a GPI-anchor defect).

4.4Proving haemolysis

  1. Fewer red cells: usually a normocytic normochromic anaemia. Exceptions: microcytic in thalassaemia and PNH (iron lost in urine); macrocytic with high reticulocytes or folate deficiency.
  2. More production: reticulocytosis (lower than expected if erythropoiesis is ineffective) and erythroid hyperplasia with a low or reversed M:E ratio.
  3. More destruction:
    • Extravascular: high indirect bilirubin, urinary urobilinogen and faecal stercobilinogen; high iron, ferritin and saturation with low TIBC; more marrow iron.
    • Intravascular: low haptoglobin and haemopexin, high LDH, haemoglobinaemia, haemoglobinuria and haemosiderinuria.
Key pointHaemosiderinuria appears a few days after the episode and lasts for weeks, so it can prove a past intravascular haemolysis when the urine is no longer red.
