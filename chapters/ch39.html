Lysosomal storage diseases

Inherited enzyme defects that fill macrophages and other cells with undigested lipid or glycogen: the general picture, then Fabry, Pompe, Gaucher and Niemann–Pick diseases, and the look-alike pseudo-Gaucher and foam cells that a haematologist must recognise.

39.1The general picture

A group of rare inherited metabolic disorders caused by defects in lysosomal function.

Pathogenesis: the substrate builds up in spleen, liver, lungs, marrow and brain, and damages cells by triggering apoptosis, changing membrane lipids (so receptors misbehave) and causing prolonged inflammation.

Clinical picture: feeding difficulty, failure to thrive, neurological signs (weakness, incoordination, seizures), hepatosplenomegaly and jaundice.

Laboratory: variable cytopenias; inclusions and vacuoles on the blood film; lysosomal inclusions and lipid-laden macrophages in marrow, spleen and liver; and the key test, enzyme activity and substrate levels in leucocytes, fluids or biopsies. Enzyme assays do not reliably detect carriers.

39.2Fabry disease (Anderson–Fabry)

  • Cause: X-linked mutation of α-galactosidase A; globotriaosylceramide builds up in endothelial cells, epithelial cells and muscle cells.
  • Clinical: unusually for an X-linked disorder, females are affected too. Early childhood: angiokeratomas and acroparaesthesia (burning limb pain). Later childhood: abnormal sweating, abdominal symptoms, lymphoedema. Then kidney, heart and brain failure; death at 40–50 years in men and 50–65 in women.
  • Diagnosis: low α-galactosidase A in leucocytes (in men), gene study (needed in women), tissue biopsy.
  • Treatment: enzyme replacement.

39.3Pompe disease (glycogen storage disease type II)

  • Cause: deficiency of acid α-glucosidase.
  • Infantile form: cardiomyopathy and floppiness, with early death. Later-onset form: muscle disease, with survival into adulthood.
  • Blood film clue: PAS-positive glycogen vacuoles in lymphocytes.
  • Treatment: enzyme replacement.

39.4Gaucher disease

Lipid storage caused by inherited deficiency of glucocerebrosidase, which splits glucosylceramide into glucose and ceramide.

  • Autosomal recessive mutation of the glucocerebrosidase gene (GBA1, chromosome 1); glucosylceramide accumulates in the lysosomes of macrophages and damages tissues.
  • Rarely, deficiency of saposin C, the enzyme's cofactor.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Low glucocerebrosidase in leucocytes or fibroblasts; the level does not predict severity.
  • Gene study for diagnosis and prenatal testing.
  • Gaucher cells in marrow or tissue: large cells with one or more eccentric nuclei and pale cytoplasm with a fibrillary, "crumpled tissue paper" look, PAS positive. They are not found in carriers. Electron microscopy: membrane-bound spindle-shaped inclusions of twisted tubules.
  • Pancytopenia, sometimes with defective platelet function and co-inherited factor XI deficiency (both common in Ashkenazi Jews).
  • Polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia in a third; MGUS in about 20%.
  • Abnormal liver tests from infiltration; low serum cholesterol (especially HDL).
  • Macrophage markers are raised: ferritin, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase, chitotriosidase and CCL18 (pulmonary and activation-regulated chemokine, PARC). Chitotriosidase and CCL18 are used to follow response to treatment.

Treatment: enzyme replacement therapy; symptomatic treatment; substrate reduction therapy; haemopoietic stem cell transplantation; gene therapy is under study.

Pseudo-Gaucher cells look like Gaucher cells but the enzyme is normal.
  • Macrophages overloaded by high cell turnover, which outstrips their glucocerebrosidase: CML, MDS, mycobacterial infection (in AIDS), thalassaemia, rarely HLH.
  • Plasma cells stuffed with crystallised M-protein: myeloma, Waldenström macroglobulinaemia.

39.5Niemann–Pick disease

  • Types A and B (common in Ashkenazi Jews): acid sphingomyelinase deficiency, autosomal recessive.
  • Type C (the commonest): mutation of NPC1 (or NPC2), which moves cholesterol out of lysosomes. Type D is a variant of C.

  • Blood film: vacuolated lymphocytes with 1–9 lipid vacuoles (cholesterol and sphingomyelin).
  • Low enzyme activity in leucocytes and fibroblasts (types A and B); gene study for diagnosis and prenatal testing.
  • Treatment: enzyme replacement (olipudase alfa) for acid sphingomyelinase deficiency; for type C, an inhibitor of glycolipid synthesis (miglustat).

Foam cells elsewhere: hyperlipidaemia (macrophages take up oxidised LDL), sickle cell disease, silicone breast implants.
