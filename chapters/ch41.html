Diagnosis of haemoglobinopathies

41.1Approach

Variants of clinical importance: HbS, HbC, HbD-Punjab, HbE, HbO-Arab, Hb Lepore, unstable haemoglobins, high-affinity haemoglobins, and HbM (methaemoglobin, a non-functional form with ferric instead of ferrous iron, also caused by oxidising poisons). The clinical side is in Chapter 5.

  1. History: family (consanguinity, haemolytic anaemia, the parents' blood counts); ethnicity, age, pregnancy; transfusion; drugs such as hydroxyurea; onset and any previous diagnosis.
  2. Clinical and radiological findings.
  3. Red cell indices and blood film.
  4. At least two separation techniques: electrophoresis, HPLC, isoelectric focusing, DNA testing.
  5. Specialised tests: mass spectrometry, oxygen dissociation curve, tests for unstable haemoglobin, Kleihauer test.

41.2Haemoglobin electrophoresis: basics

Separation of haemoglobins in an electric field.

Indications: unexplained haemolytic anaemia; microcytosis not due to iron deficiency, chronic disease or lead; abnormal red cells such as sickle cells; a positive sickling or solubility test; a family history; premarital screening.

Principle: globins are zwitterions, positive in acid and negative in alkaline buffer. Haemoglobins move at different speeds according to their charge and size.

  • Sample: EDTA blood, stable for 7 days at 4 °C; longer storage denatures haemoglobin. Dried blood spots suit HPLC and isoelectric focusing only.
  • Control: equal parts of HbS trait, HbC trait and cord blood, or HbSC with cord blood.
  • Buffers: alkaline Tris–EDTA–borate (stored at 4 °C, usable up to 10 times); acid citrate buffer.

41.3Alkaline electrophoresis (cellulose acetate, pH 8.4–8.6)

All haemoglobins are negative and run towards the anode; the sample is applied at the cathode end.

  • Variants whose substitution does not change charge cannot be separated.
  • Carbonic anhydrase, the second most abundant red cell protein, gives a faint band near the origin that must not be counted.
  • It is a key primary screening method, second only to HPLC.
  • Technical errors: wrong amount applied, wrong origin position, delay before running or staining, poor blotting.
  • Unresolved problems: HbE vs Hb E-Saskatoon; HbD-Punjab vs other D and G haemoglobins.

41.4Acid electrophoresis (citrate agar, pH 6.0–6.2)

  • Haemoglobins move partly by charge and partly by interaction with the agar (electro-endosmosis). The sample is applied centrally.
  • Method: cool with frozen sticks or run at 4 °C. Dissolve 0.5 g agar in 50 mL buffer at 95 °C, cool to 60 °C, add potassium cyanide, pour into trays and let set. Run at 50 V for 60 min, stain, wash in 3% acetic acid, dry on Gel Bond.
  • Band order: F – A – S – C.
  • Run with HbA: HbA₂, HbD, HbE, Hb Lepore, HbG, Hb Osu-Christiansborg, HbH, HbI, HbN, Hb E-Saskatoon. Run with HbF: Hb Bart's, Hb Hasharon, Hb C-Harlem. HbO-Arab sits in the S region but closer to the origin.
  • Value: it separates HbS from HbD and HbG, and HbC from HbE and HbO-Arab. It matters most when the variant is a small percentage, as in newborn screening, where HbS may be too low for a positive solubility test even in a homozygote.

41.5Isoelectric focusing

  • A stable pH gradient (6–8) lies between the electrodes; each haemoglobin stops at its isoelectric point as a very sharp band. Bands anodal to HbA are degradation products of an old sample.
  • Advantages: works on haemolysate, whole blood or dried spots; separates more variants than cellulose acetate; resolves HbC from HbE and HbS from HbD; separates HbF from HbA and from HbS, C, D-Punjab, E and O-Arab; can be semi-automated for screening.
  • Disadvantages: post-translational forms (oxidised and glycated HbA, acetylated HbF) form extra bands; HbC, HbE and HbA₂ share mobility; not validated for HbA₂ quantification.

41.6Capillary electrophoresis

  • Electrophoresis in buffer-filled silica capillaries 25–100 µm wide, allowing high voltages and short runs. Separation is by electro-osmotic flow and electrophoretic mobility at pH 9.4; detection at 415 nm.
  • Migration depends mainly on charge to size: more charge, faster and shorter time; bigger, slower. Buffer, pH, ionic strength, field and capillary length also matter.
  • Fresh EDTA blood, at least 1 mL, up to 7 days at 2–8 °C. Fully automated: barcode, mixing, haemolysis and dilution, washing, injection, detection.
  • The trace is divided into 15 zones anchored on HbA. If there is no HbA, rerun mixed 1:1 with a normal control for identification only; quantify from the original run.
  • Advantages: tiny sample (good for newborns); excellent precision for A, F, A₂ and common variants; post-translational forms are not separated; separates HbA₂ from HbE and from Hb Lepore; HbA₂ is more accurate than HPLC when HbS or HbD is present; quantifies Hb Bart's and HbH.
  • Disadvantages: HbA₂ partly overlaps HbC; HbF may be overestimated in diabetes (glycated HbA overlaps HbF), and diabetes cannot be diagnosed; HbD, Lepore and G-Philadelphia move alike; rare variants may hide in HbA; no HbA means no zones.
  • Examples: β thalassaemia trait, HbA 95.3% with HbA₂ 4.7%; sickle trait, HbA 57.4%, HbS 39%, HbA₂ 3.3%.

41.7Cation-exchange HPLC

High-performance liquid chromatography separates haemoglobins between a stationary phase (negatively charged carboxyl groups on 5 µm silica) and a mobile phase (phosphate buffers whose ionic strength rises steadily). Haemoglobins swap places with buffer cations and elute in turn, from Hb Bart's (shortest) to the α₂ᴳβ₂ᶜ hybrid (longest).

  • Parts: solvent reservoir, pump, injector, column, UV/visible detector, computer that turns peaks into percentages.
  • Sample: EDTA whole blood, one week at 2–8 °C; about 5 µL is enough.
  • Retention time depends on the haemoglobin, buffer pH and ionic strength, column age, mobile phase and temperature. Relative retention time (variant ÷ HbA) compares better between runs.

Quality checks before reporting

  • Total area 1–3 million. Anaemic samples: concentrate by removing plasma; polycythaemic: dilute.
  • HbA1c and HbA0 in range and correctly named; HbA1c peak sharp and symmetrical; baseline flat, stable, not drifting.
  • HbF and HbA₂ calibration factors 0.7–1.3.
  • Always inspect the chromatogram: software can misname peaks, and some programs (for example the β-thalassaemia short program) do not integrate the first 30 seconds, hiding HbH, Hb Bart's and acetylated HbF.
  • Carry-over after a sample with HbS or HbC; carry-over of HbE falsely raises HbA₂.

41.8Mass spectrometry and DNA tests

  • Mass spectrometry (electrospray ionisation, MALDI-TOF) identifies variants by mass; its very low cost per test suits newborn screening with HPLC and capillary electrophoresis.
  • Nucleic acid testing detects the mutation itself. Indications: diagnosis not confirmed by other methods; the mutation matters for management; genetic counselling; premarital and prenatal testing. It also helps predict severity.
  • Methods: reverse hybridisation, real-time PCR, MLPA (for deletions), array CGH, DNA sequencing.
