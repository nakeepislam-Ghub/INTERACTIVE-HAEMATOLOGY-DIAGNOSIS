8.1Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA)

Destruction of the patient's own red cells by autoantibodies, with a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test.

  • Warm AIHA (80–90% of adult cases), cold agglutinin disease (~15%), paroxysmal cold haemoglobinuria, mixed warm and cold, and drug-induced.
  • Mechanisms: complement activation to the membrane attack complex (intravascular), opsonisation (extravascular), or both. IgG-coated cells are taken up mainly in the spleen; cells heavily coated with complement (C3b) mainly in the liver, which has the most C3b receptors.
  • Severity depends on the rate of destruction and on how well the marrow compensates (folate deficiency, infiltration and anti-reticulocyte antibodies all limit it).

Clinical and laboratory features

  • Any age, more in women of 40–50; usually gradual, relapsing and remitting. Look for drugs, lymphoproliferative and autoimmune disease.
  • Cold agglutinin disease: acrocyanosis in the cold that clears on warming. PCH: cold urticaria.
  • Film: spherocytes and microspherocytes, polychromasia, red cell agglutination (false high MCV in cold disease), nucleated red cells, erythrophagocytosis.
  • Evans syndrome = warm AIHA with immune thrombocytopenia (± neutropenia); steroid-resistant.
  • DAT with anti-IgG and anti-C3d (see the interpreter); eluates show specificity; cold agglutinin titre (anti-I or anti-i); Donath–Landsteiner test for PCH.
  • Positive DAT without haemolysis: myeloma, SLE, methyldopa, cephalosporins.

8.3Infection, chemicals and physical agents

  • Malaria: marrow suppression, dyserythropoiesis, hypersplenism and sequestration, folate deficiency, haemolysis (blackwater fever), quinine immune haemolysis or oxidant damage in G6PD deficiency.
  • Clostridium perfringens toxins: intense intravascular haemolysis with microspherocytes. Babesiosis is severe after splenectomy. Bartonellosis: intravascular haemolysis with erythrophagocytosis.
  • Oxidant drugs (dapsone, sulphasalazine): chronic haemolysis with bite cells and Heinz bodies; nitrites and nitrates cause methaemoglobinaemia (treat with methylene blue or ascorbic acid). Chlorates and arsine: acute haemolysis with renal failure.
  • Burns: intravascular haemolysis in the first 24 h with spherocytes and fragments. Also lead, Wilson disease and drowning.

8.4Red cell fragmentation syndromes

  • Cardiac: prosthetic valves and grafts, especially paravalvular leaks; chronic loss in the urine leads to iron deficiency.
  • Vascular malformations: Kasabach–Merritt syndrome (giant haemangioma, platelet trapping and DIC).
  • Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA): DIC, HUS, TTP, malignant hypertension, pre-eclampsia and HELLP, SLE, vasculitis, transplant rejection, mitomycin C, ciclosporin, mucin-secreting carcinoma.
  • Laboratory: anaemia with thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, reticulocytosis, negative DAT, intravascular haemolysis markers; coagulation tests for DIC.

8.5Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH)

An acquired, clonal stem cell disorder: a somatic mutation of the X-linked PIGA gene blocks the GPI anchor, so CD55 (DAF) and CD59 (MIRL) are missing and complement lyses the cells. It may arise alone or with aplastic anaemia.

  • Haemolysis (intravascular, worse at night and with infection): anaemia, dark morning urine, persistent haemosiderinuria. Free Hb consumes nitric oxide: abdominal pain, oesophageal spasm, erectile dysfunction, fatigue.
  • Thrombosis, the commonest cause of death: hepatic (Budd–Chiari), portal, mesenteric, cerebral sinus and skin veins.
  • Marrow failure and progression to aplastic anaemia, MDS or AML.
  • Renal failure from haemoglobin, iron and vein thrombosis.

Laboratory

  • Anaemia or pancytopenia; MCV high from reticulocytes or low from iron deficiency (urinary iron loss); low NAP score; very high LDH; haemosiderinuria; marrow erythroid hyperplasia or hypoplasia.
  • Flow cytometry is the test of choice: loss of CD55 and CD59 on red cells (type III complete, type II partial) and loss of GPI-linked markers on neutrophils (more sensitive: no transfusion dilution). FLAER on granulocytes is the most sensitive and specific.
  • Older tests: Ham (acidified serum) test and sucrose lysis test. HEMPAS (CDA II) also gives a positive Ham test but only with normal serum, and a negative sucrose test.
  • Suspect PNH with haemolysis plus cytopenias, dark urine, abdominal pain or thrombosis.
  • Treatment: eculizumab (anti-C5; vaccinate against meningococcus at least 2 weeks before) or allogeneic transplant.

Dark urine: what is it?

  • Haematuria clears on centrifugation; myoglobin (rhabdomyolysis) separates on ultracentrifugation.
  • Haemoglobinuria: transfusion reaction (DAT positive), PCH (Donath–Landsteiner), G6PD (assay after the attack), blackwater fever (malaria film), clostridia (culture), march haemoglobinuria (history), MAHA (schistocytes), PNH (CD55/CD59).
