Investigating haematological malignancies

The biology behind blood cancers (mutations, epigenetics, the cell cycle, apoptosis and clonality), then the tools: marrow aspirate and trephine, cytochemistry, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, PCR, sequencing and hybridisation methods, and the detection of minimal residual disease.

43.2Mutations and epigenetics

  • Normally, sequence errors are caught at cell cycle checkpoints and repaired, or the cell dies by apoptosis. A mutation escapes and is passed to the progeny.
  • Germline mutations are in sperm or ova and pass to every cell of the offspring; somatic (acquired) mutations arise in body cells and are not inherited.
  • Causes: spontaneous (chemical instability, such as deamination of cytosine to uracil; replication errors) or induced (ultraviolet light, chemicals).
  • Site: in the coding sequence (may or may not change the amino acid) or outside it: a new splice site gives abnormal mRNA; an enhancer or promoter change lowers output.
MechanismTypes

InversionA segment cut, turned and reinserted (inv(16))

Epigenetic mechanisms: DNA methylation of cytosine silences genes; histone acetylation loosens DNA and allows transcription.

43.3The cell cycle

  • About 24 hours on average. Cyclins are made at set stages and activate cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), then are degraded.
  • CDK inhibitors are tumour suppressors: the CIP/KIP family (p21, activated by p53 after DNA damage; p27, activated by TGF-β; p57) and the INK4a/ARF family (p14ARF, p15, p16, p18, p19). INK proteins hold CDK4 and CDK6; ARF stops p53 being degraded.
  • Checkpoint kinases: ATM (DNA damage) and ATR (ultraviolet and ionising radiation).
PhaseEvents and control
G0Resting; growth signals move cells into G1. Some cells never divide (neurones), some rarely (liver, kidney), some constantly (epithelia).
G1Growth. Cyclin D with CDK4/6 phosphorylates Rb, freeing E2F to switch on S-phase genes (MYC drives E2F); cyclin A or E with CDK2 completes it. Restriction point: DNA damage activates ATM/ATR, which phosphorylate p53, releasing it from MDM2; p53 induces p21, arresting the cycle for repair, or apoptosis if repair fails.
SDNA replication and centrosome duplication
G2Growth and spindle formation. Cyclin B/CDK1 (mitosis-promoting factor) is held off by phosphorylation of Thr14 and Tyr15 until repair is done, then CDC25 removes it.
M1–2 hours: prophase (chromosomes condense), metaphase (alignment; the spindle checkpoint; the anaphase-promoting complex degrades securin, freeing separase to cut cohesin), anaphase (chromatids separate), telophase (nuclei reform)

43.4Apoptosis and malignant transformation

  • Apoptosis is programmed cell death that keeps tissues in balance: it removes damaged, infected and self-reactive cells and prevents cancer. Too much causes hypoplasia; too little allows cancer.
  • Intrinsic pathway (heat, growth factor loss, steroids, viruses, hypoxia, radiation, cytotoxics): the mitochondrial membrane leaks cytochrome c, which with Apaf-1 and ATP forms the apoptosome, activating caspase 9 then caspase 3; SMAC blocks the inhibitors of apoptosis. BCL2 proteins suppress it.
  • Death receptor pathway: FAS (CD95) with FAS ligand forms the death-inducing signalling complex (FADD, caspases 8 and 10; see ALPS in Chapter 37); TNF-R1 via TRADD and FADD; DR4 via TRAIL (decoy receptors activate NF-κB instead).
  • Dying cells expose phosphatidylserine and are eaten without inflammation. They bleb, shrink, condense, show pyknosis and karyorrhexis.
  • Neoplastic expansion often reflects too little apoptosis rather than fast division.
Malignant transformation needs genomic instability with several mutations and epigenetic changes in proto-oncogenes (normally drive growth: growth factors and receptors, signalling proteins, cyclins, CDKs, apoptosis inhibitors, transcription factors), activated by translocation, amplification or point mutation, and in tumour suppressor genes, which must be lost on both alleles (deletion, frameshift, promoter silencing, inactivating point mutation). Gatekeepers brake the cycle; caretakers repair DNA and keep the genome stable. The result: uncontrolled growth, skipped checkpoints, blocked differentiation and resistance to apoptosis.

Evidence of clonality: the same acquired genetic change in all cells (karyotype or molecular); antigen receptor gene rearrangement; a single X-inactivation pattern (one G6PD isoenzyme, or X-linked RFLP methylation); immunophenotype (such as light chain restriction).

43.5Bone marrow examination

Investigations of a suspected neoplasm: blood count, film, marrow aspirate and trephine, cytochemistry, immunophenotyping and genetic tests. Specimens: needle aspirate, trephine biopsy, open surgical biopsy.

PurposeExamples
DiagnosisMyeloma, aleukaemic leukaemia, aplastic anaemia, bone metastases, myelofibrosis, storage disease, megaloblastic anaemia
ConfirmationLeukaemia, leishmaniasis, hypersplenism, ITP
PrognosisLymphoma staging, minimal residual disease
Differential diagnosisPancytopenia; infectious mononucleosis vs leukaemia; CML vs leucoerythroblastic anaemia or leukaemoid reaction; hypochromic anaemia (Perls' stain)
TransplantationAplastic anaemia, agranulocytosis, some acute leukaemias, immunodeficiency
  • Complications: sternum: laceration of vessels or heart (soft bone in myeloma), pneumothorax, sternomanubrial separation. Any site: haemorrhage (rarely retroperitoneal or into the thigh), lateral cutaneous nerve injury, infection, needle breakage in osteosclerosis, avulsion fracture, tumour seeding (plasmacytoma, lymphoma), serous leak in nephrotic syndrome, exostosis.

Aspirate

  • Sites: sternum at the second intercostal space (easy but painful, risky, unsuitable for young children); ilium, anterior or posterior superior iliac spine (aspirate and trephine through one incision; the posterior spine also suits children); medial tibia in infants under 18 months.
  • Spread quickly (it clots) and anticoagulate the rest. Uses: wedge and crush films, sections of fragments, electron microscopy, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, culture.
  • Strengths: fine cytology; more cytochemistry, flow and genetics; enough alone for iron deficiency, chronic disease, megaloblastic anaemia and acute leukaemia. Weaknesses: blood dilution, dry or blood taps, must be fresh, shows only haemopoietic cells.
  • Dry tap: poor technique; packed marrow (leukaemia, lymphoma); metastatic cancer; MPN with fibrosis or osteosclerosis; myelofibrosis; hairy cell leukaemia; granulomas. A blood tap also suggests fibrosis.
  • Reading: 3–4 films; ×10 for cellularity, megakaryocytes and infiltrates; ×100 for detail, a 500-cell differential and Perls' stain. Count trails behind fragments; plasma cells and lymphocytes are patchy. Normal cellularity 25–75%, falling with age (lumbar vertebrae about 10% more than iliac crest). Diluted samples have more lymphocytes and mature granulocytes.

Aspirate report: clinical details, blood count and film; site, bone texture and ease; cellularity; M:E ratio; each lineage; iron in fragments and cells; whether a trephine was taken; flow, cytogenetic or molecular tests; conclusion and advice.

Trephine biopsy

  • Posterior superior iliac spine preferred. Decalcified paraffin or undecalcified resin sections. Core at least 20–30 mm (5–6 intertrabecular spaces): small cores look hypocellular, and decalcification shrinks cellularity by about 5%.
  • Definite indications: lymphoma staging; hairy cell leukaemia; CLL prognosis; suspected myeloma; MPN; aplastic anaemia and hypoplastic MDS or AML; unexplained leucoerythroblastic picture; metastatic carcinoma; small round cell tumours of childhood; fever of unknown origin; bone disease; failed aspirate. Possible: AML, MDS, Hodgkin staging, CML, amyloidosis.
  • Also gives touch imprints, electron microscopy, immunohistochemistry and frozen sections. Never fails, shows architecture and pattern of infiltration, and blocks keep indefinitely; more painful.
  • Look at: bone (trabeculae, osteoblasts, osteoclasts); erythroid islands and dysplasia; granulocyte position (abnormally located immature precursors); megakaryocyte number, clusters and morphology; lymphoid aggregates and plasma cells; macrophages (haemophagocytosis, organisms, Gaucher cells, granulomas); mast cells; reticulin; iron; immunohistochemistry. Giemsa helps identify mast cells and plasma cells and separate early erythroid cells from myeloblasts.
  • Reticulin grading: 0 none; 1 fine fibres; 2 fine network through most of the section; 3 diffuse network with thick coarse fibres; 4 coarse network with collagen. Normal 0–1 (sometimes 2), around vessels and trabeculae. Silver stain for reticulin; trichrome (Martius scarlet blue better than van Gieson) for collagen. Focal increase suggests granulomas, carcinoma or lymphoma.
  • Iron grading (resin): 0 none; 1+ tiny particles at oil immersion; 2+ sparse small particles at low power; 3+ many small particles; 4+ larger particles clumping; 5+ dense large clumps; 6+ very large clumps with extracellular iron. Decalcified sections: absent, present or increased.

43.6Cytochemistry

43.7Immunophenotyping

  • Flow cytometry on viable or fixed cell suspensions; immunocytochemistry on cytospins or films; immunohistochemistry on frozen or paraffin sections, using enzyme-linked antibodies. Immunohistochemistry is needed for antibodies that do not work in flow, or where the pattern matters, such as membrane and Golgi CD30 and CD15 in classic Hodgkin lymphoma.
  • Principles, gating and panels are in Chapter 26 and Chapter 27.

43.8Cytogenetics

  • Chromosomes are identified at metaphase by size, arm ratio, centromere position and banding. Metaphases come from direct preparation (fast-growing tumours) or short culture, with or without mitogens.
  • Method: colcemid arrests mitosis; hypotonic saline swells the cells; methanol–acetic acid fixes; spread, heat or protease treatment, then Giemsa or fluorescent banding.
  • Samples (fresh, within 24 hours, cell suspensions only): marrow in heparin or culture medium; blood in heparin or EDTA (few dividing cells); PHA-stimulated lymphocytes; nodes and other tissue; effusions; skin fibroblasts; amniocytes.
  • Uses: diagnosis, clonality, finding the lesion, classification, prognosis, monitoring, residual disease, and separating therapy-related leukaemia after alkylators from that after topoisomerase II inhibitors.
  • Limits: needs dividing cells, so fails in slow-growing disease; complex changes are not fully resolved.

43.9Molecular techniques

Methods to identify, isolate and manipulate DNA, RNA and proteins: extract the nucleic acid, detect it, then discriminate the result. The melting temperature (Tm) is the temperature at which half the DNA is single-stranded.

  • Uses: as for cytogenetics, plus clonality. Beware lineage infidelity: 5–10% of mature and most precursor B-cell neoplasms carry clonal TCR rearrangements, and the reverse in T-cell disease; some myeloid neoplasms have them too.
  • Advantages over cytogenetics: no dividing cells needed; finds fusion transcripts, point mutations, antigen receptor rearrangements and somatic hypermutation (unmutated = pre-germinal centre; hypermutated with ongoing mutation = germinal centre; hypermutated without = post-germinal centre); works on stained films; scans many cells for small populations; fast.
  • DNA extraction: lyse cells with chemicals that dissolve membranes and denature proteins, chelate the nucleases' cations, remove proteins (organic solvents, salt precipitation, affinity columns) and precipitate DNA. From whole blood, buffy coat, marrow or tissue.
  • Detection: UV absorbance at 260 nm (single strands absorb more); fluorescent dyes (ethidium bromide, SYBR Green) intercalate and are about 10 000 times more sensitive.
  • Discrimination: agarose or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (pulsed-field for over 50 kb), HPLC and mass spectrometry, hybridisation.

PCR

  • Amplifies one or a few copies of a DNA target millions of times. Needs template, two primers (complementary to the 3′ ends of each strand), a heat-stable DNA polymerase (about 70 °C optimum), dNTPs, buffer, Mg²⁺ or Mn²⁺, and K⁺.
  • 20–40 cycles of denaturation (94–98 °C), annealing (50–65 °C) and extension (72 °C), after an initial heating step and before a final elongation. Products are sized on agarose gel beside a no-template blank. Step through it in the PCR stepper.
  • Variants: radiolabelled; multiplex (several targets of different sizes); nested (a second primer pair inside the first product, for specificity); automated fragment analysis of fluorescent products; in-situ PCR on tissue sections; reverse transcription PCR for fusion transcripts; quantitative real-time PCR.
  • Better than Southern blot: fast, no radioisotopes, tiny amounts of DNA, works on fixed and archival tissue, paraffin trephines, even scraped films.

Sequencing

  • Sanger sequencing: chain termination by labelled dideoxynucleotides lacking a 3′-OH. Used when many different mutations can cause one disorder.
  • Next-generation sequencing: millions of fragments in parallel. Take the diagnostic sample to define the clone for later MRD tracking. It can miss large deletions, fusions and variants below the cut-off, and cannot easily separate somatic from germline: a variant allele frequency near 50% suggests germline, but proof needs tumour and normal tissue.

Hybridisation methods

A labelled single-stranded probe (genomic, cloned or synthetic oligonucleotide of 15–45 bases; radioactive, fluorescent, biotin or digoxigenin) anneals to its complementary sequence after DNA is denatured by heat or high pH, on a solid phase (nitrocellulose or nylon) or, more efficiently, in solution.

43.10Minimal residual disease

Neoplastic cells below the level of morphology at set times during or after treatment, found by sensitive methods. It measures response and predicts relapse.
