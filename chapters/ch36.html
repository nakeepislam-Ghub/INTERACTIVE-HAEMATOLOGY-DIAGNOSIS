Eosinophil disorders

How eosinophils are made, recruited and armed, what lowers or raises them, how to separate reactive from clonal eosinophilia, and the idiopathic hypereosinophilic syndrome.

36.1Eosinophils

  • Development: stem cell → myeloid progenitor → eosinophil under IL-3, GM-CSF and above all IL-5 (made by T cells, mast cells, macrophages, NK cells, endothelial and stromal cells) → blood → tissues exposed to the outside world (gut, lung, skin).
  • Recruitment: C5a, platelet-activating factor, IL-3, IL-5, GM-CSF, IL-2, RANTES, eotaxins and lymphocyte chemoattractant factor from CD8+ T cells.
  • Degranulation: triggered by antigens, cytokines, complexed or secretory IgA, IgE and IgG. It leads to histamine release from basophils and mast cells, amplifying inflammation, and to tissue injury.

Functions:

  1. Cytotoxic: eosinophil peroxidase is more bactericidal than MPO, but the main target is metazoan parasites.
  2. Pro-inflammatory: allergic, parasitic and malignant disease.
  3. Tissue repair and remodelling: deposition of extracellular matrix proteins.

36.2Eosinopenia

Cushing syndrome, corticosteroid therapy, acromegaly and acute infection (stress).

36.3Eosinophilia

An eosinophil count above 0.5 ×10⁹/L. Hypereosinophilia is above 1.5 ×10⁹/L.

Reactive (the commonest)

InfectionParasites: filariasis, strongyloidiasis, hydatid disease, onchocerciasis, visceral larva migrans. Others: coccidioidomycosis, recovery from acute infection, cat scratch disease, cryptococcosis

PulmonaryAspergillosis, eosinophilic pneumonia, tropical pulmonary eosinophilia, Löffler syndrome, Churg–Strauss syndrome (eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis)

Connective tissueVasculitis, serum sickness, eosinophilic fasciitis

Neoplastic: eosinophils are part of the malignant clone

  • Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia (eosinophils 10–50%).
  • CMML or atypical CML with eosinophilia.
  • MPN with eosinophilia and T-lymphoblastic lymphoma: ZNF198::FGFR1 fusion.
  • CML; AML with inv(16); occasionally ALL.
  • The tyrosine kinase fusions (PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, FLT3, ETV6::ABL1) are covered in Chapter 19.

Miscellaneous

  • Eosinophilia–myalgia syndrome: L-tryptophan intake causing blood eosinophilia, fatigue and severe myalgia; it settles once the tryptophan is stopped.
  • Toxic oil syndrome.
  • Gleich syndrome: cyclic eosinophilia with angio-oedema.
  • Familial eosinophilia: autosomal dominant, linked to chromosome 5.
  • Idiopathic hypereosinophilic syndrome (below).

36.4Idiopathic hypereosinophilic syndrome

Definition: eosinophils 30–70% of white cells (over 1.5 ×10⁹/L) for more than 6 months, with no cause found, abnormal eosinophils, and 30–60% eosinophils in the marrow without an increase in myeloblasts.

Pathogenesis: eosinophil granules degranulate and release mediators such as eosinophil cationic protein and major basic protein, which damage organs.

Diagnosis:

  1. Cause: family, drug and travel history; routine chemistry; serum IgE; vitamin B12 (high in myeloid neoplasms); HIV serology; stool for ova and parasites (and Strongyloides serology); marrow aspirate and trephine; cytogenetics and molecular tests for a clonal TCR rearrangement, BCR::ABL1, FIP1L1::PDGFRA and FGFR1 rearrangement.
  2. Organ damage: full examination, ECG and echocardiogram, pulmonary function tests, CT of chest and abdomen.

Treatment: corticosteroids; cytotoxic agents; intravenous immunoglobulin; for FIP1L1::PDGFRA (or PDGFRB) rearranged disease, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor active against PDGFRα and PDGFRβ, such as imatinib, which works even at low doses.
