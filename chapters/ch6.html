The membrane is about 50% protein, 40% lipid (phospholipids, 25–30% cholesterol, glycolipids) and 10% carbohydrate. Phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin sit in the outer leaflet; phosphatidylserine, ethanolamine and inositol in the inner leaflet. Sialic acid gives most of the negative surface charge.

6.2Hereditary spherocytosis

The commonest inherited haemolytic anaemia in people of northern European origin: a chronic, intrinsic, extravascular haemolysis.

  • Defects of vertical links: spectrin–ankyrin (60%), band 3 (25%), band 4.2 and others.
  • Inheritance: mostly autosomal dominant (family history in 75%); recessive forms are rarer and more severe.
  1. Normal discs lose bits of the outer leaflet: the surface area to volume ratio falls and cells become spheres.
  2. "Splenic conditioning" removes more membrane: rigid microspherocytes are destroyed in the spleen.
  3. Leaky membranes make the Na⁺/K⁺ pump work harder, using up ATP and glucose.
  • Clinical: from no symptoms to neonatal jaundice (days 5–30) and variable anaemia; aplastic (parvovirus), megaloblastic and haemolytic crises; pigment gallstones; rarely leg ulcers.
  • Blood: normocytic anaemia, high MCHC (dehydrated cells), spherocytes and polychromasia, reticulocytosis.
  • DAT negative: this separates it from warm autoimmune haemolysis.
  • Osmotic fragility increased, more after 24 h incubation; a tail of resistant reticulocytes may appear.
  • EMA binding by flow cytometry is reduced (eosin-5-maleimide binds band 3); also the glycerol lysis and acidified glycerol lysis times, cryohaemolysis test and SDS-PAGE of membrane proteins.
  • Autohaemolysis (48 h at 37 °C) is increased and corrected by glucose.
  • Treatment: folic acid; splenectomy in significant cases cures the anaemia, although spherocytes remain.

6.3Hereditary elliptocytosis and pyropoikilocytosis

Defects in α or β spectrin (self-association), protein 4.1 or glycophorin C make rigid elliptocytes or heat-sensitive, fragmented pyropoikilocytes.

  • Silent carrier: no anaemia.
  • Common HE: no or compensated haemolysis; worse with infection, pregnancy or folate lack.
  • Neonatal poikilocytosis that settles in the first year.
  • Spherocytic HE: β-spectrin defects, spherocytes and elliptocytes, mild to moderate haemolysis.
  • HPP: recessive (homozygous or compound HE alleles), severe; cells fragment above 46 °C.
  • South-East Asian ovalocytosis: band 3 deletion; rigid stomatocytic ovalocytes; protects against malaria.

Laboratory: 50–90% elliptocytes on the film of the patient and relatives; osmotic fragility up only in spherocytic HE and HPP. Exclude other causes of elliptocytes: iron deficiency, megaloblastic anaemia, thalassaemia, myelofibrosis, leucoerythroblastic anaemia and liver disease. Treat with folate and splenectomy if severe.

6.4Hereditary stomatocytosis

  • Rare, dominant cation-leak defects: dehydrated (xerocytosis, high MCHC, perinatal ascites), overhydrated (low MCHC), cryohydrocytosis and familial pseudohyperkalaemia.
  • Film: macrocytes and 10–30% stomatocytes; osmotic fragility changed; high intracellular Na⁺; pseudohyperkalaemia from K⁺ leaking at room temperature.
  • Avoid splenectomy: high risk of thrombosis.
  • Rh-null syndrome: no Rh complex, mild haemolysis with stomatocytes.

6.5Lipid and acquired membrane disorders

  • Acanthocytes (spur cells): end-stage liver disease, malnutrition, hypothyroidism; abetalipoproteinaemia (with retinitis pigmentosa and fat malabsorption); chorea-acanthocytosis; McLeod phenotype (weak Kell, X-linked).
  • LCAT deficiency: target cells or acanthocytes.
  • Liver disease: target cells in obstructive jaundice; Zieve syndrome (alcohol, hyperlipidaemia, haemolysis); Wilson disease with acute haemolysis.
  • Vitamin E deficiency: oxidant damage.
  • Acquired spherocytes: warm AIHA, transfusion, clostridial toxin, burns, ABO haemolytic disease of the newborn, Wilson disease, old samples.
  • Non-spherocytic haemolysis: PK and G6PD deficiency, PNH, Rh haemolytic disease, unstable haemoglobins.
