11.1Pancytopenia: definition and causes

A fall of all three blood cell lines below the age-adjusted normal ranges. It comes either from less production (defective stem cells or missing growth factors) or from immune or non-immune destruction in the blood and spleen.

11.2Approach to pancytopenia

Work through the Yes/No pancytopenia work-up below the lecture.

History and examination

  • Family history (inherited marrow failure, familial HLH), recurrence, drugs, diet and social background.
  • Onset and course: acute in leukaemia and infection, chronic otherwise; transient after viruses and chemotherapy.
  • Symptoms of each cytopenia: anaemia (pallor, fatigue, dizziness), leucopenia (infections), thrombocytopenia (petechiae, bleeding).
  • Pointers: fever and splenomegaly (HLH); left upper quadrant fullness (hypersplenism); jaundice and ascites (liver failure); abdominal pain, dark urine and odd thromboses (PNH); glossitis and neuropathy (B12); bone pain (leukaemia, myelofibrosis, myeloma); sore throat (mononucleosis); chest disease (TB); rash, arthritis and nephritis (SLE); transfusion or transplant with rash and diarrhoea (GVHD); a known cancer (infiltration).
  • Examine for cachexia, somatic anomalies, nodes, liver and spleen, oral candida, purpura, bone tenderness and chest signs. Ultrasound or CT for organomegaly, nodes and focal lesions.

Laboratory

  1. Repeat the blood count on a fresh sample before calling it pancytopenia. Anaemia is macrocytic in megaloblastic anaemia, some aplastic anaemia, liver disease and MDS; microcytic in PNH (urinary iron loss) and in myelofibrosis with bleeding. Relative lymphocytosis is common (long-lived lymphocytes).
  2. Film: teardrops and anisopoikilocytosis (myelofibrosis, MDS); oval macrocytes and hypersegmentation (megaloblastic); spherocytes (immune haemolysis); acanthocytes (liver); blasts; pseudo-Pelger and hypogranular neutrophils (MDS); hairy cells; reactive lymphocytes; giant platelets (MDS, hypersplenism); leucoerythroblastic change; haemophagocytosis by monocytes.
  3. Reticulocyte production index: low in aplasia, leukaemia, MDS, HLH and infection; high-normal in hypersplenism.
  4. Bone marrow aspirate and trephine: see the table below.
  5. Iron and ferritin (high in aplasia, HLH, MDS, megaloblastic anaemia; low in PNH), bilirubin, liver and renal function, urine for haemoglobin.
  6. Flow cytometry (leukaemia, LPD, hairy cells, CD55/CD59 for PNH), TRAP, serum and urine electrophoresis, B12 and folate, ANA and anti-dsDNA, viral serology (HBV, HCV, HIV, CMV, EBV), tuberculin and ZN, leishmania antibodies, cytogenetics and gene tests, diepoxybutane breakage test (Fanconi), node or splenic biopsy, enzyme assay for storage disease.

Treatment is of the cause: growth factors and stem cell transplant (aplasia, inherited failure), chemotherapy and transplant (leukaemia, MDS, myelofibrosis, infiltration), splenectomy (hypersplenism), eculizumab or transplant (PNH), B12 and folate, steroids and immunosuppression (SLE), antituberculous drugs, liposomal amphotericin B or miltefosine (leishmaniasis).

11.3Bone marrow failure: general features

  • Definition: cytopenia because the marrow fails to produce mature cells, from defects in haemopoietic stem cell division or survival.
  • Three age peaks: 2–5 years (inherited failure commonest), 20–25 years, and over 65 (acquired failure commonest).
  • Clinical: symptoms of cytopenia; somatic anomalies and a raised risk of blood and solid cancers in inherited forms.
  • Laboratory: cytopenias, low reticulocytes, possible dysplasia in inherited forms. The marrow is markedly hypocellular, replaced by fat and stroma; residual cells are mostly lymphocytes; sometimes only one line is affected.
  • Exclude malignancy (blasts, cytogenetics), hypoplastic MDS (dysplasia), megaloblastic anaemia (B12, serum and red cell folate), a PNH clone (CD55/CD59, Ham test, high reticulocytes, low NAP), viruses (hepatitis A, B, C, EBV, CMV, HIV), autoimmune disease (ANA) and liver disease.

Management: haemopoietic stem cell transplant is the only cure (immunosuppression does not help; gene therapy may in future). Screen first-degree relatives and offer genetic counselling. Support with infection prophylaxis, leucodepleted red cells for Hb under 7 g/dL, platelets under 10 ×10⁹/L (or under 50 when bleeding), avoiding family donors to limit alloimmunisation, and chelation for iron overload. EPO and G-CSF are not recommended: there are too few precursors to respond.

Fanconi anaemia

  • Autosomal recessive mutations in the FA core complex of DNA repair proteins (13 genes): genomic instability, chromosome breakage and hypersensitivity to cross-linking agents (diepoxybutane, mitomycin C); also oxygen sensitivity, fast telomere shortening and TNF-α overproduction via MAPK.
  • Progressive marrow failure at 5–10 years (or later, up to 40): anaemia and thrombocytopenia first, then a hypocellular marrow with macrophage activation. Death from bleeding or infection.
  • Cancer: AML (especially monocytic types) and solid tumours: liver, squamous cancers of vulva, oesophagus, head and neck; Wilms tumour in children, breast cancer in adults.
  • Anomalies: skeletal (thumbs and radii), short stature, café-au-lait or hypo- and hyperpigmented skin, microphthalmia, deafness, hydrocephalus, learning difficulty, renal, genital, gut and heart malformations; a third have none.
  • Tests: increased chromosome breakage in PHA-stimulated lymphocytes or fibroblasts exposed to diepoxybutane or mitomycin C; G2 arrest in melphalan-dosed cells; confirm by gene sequencing.

Dyskeratosis congenita

  • Defects of telomere maintenance: X-linked DKC1 (dyskerin), autosomal dominant TERC (hTR, telomerase RNA) and recessive forms. Short telomeres exhaust stem cells; metaphases show rearrangements rather than breaks.
  • Marrow failure may come before the skin signs and be labelled idiopathic aplastic anaemia. High risk of blood and solid cancers.
  • Classic triad: reticulate skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy and oral leukoplakia; also early grey hair and hair loss, pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, osteoporosis, short stature, developmental delay, epiphora, urethral stricture.
  • Tests: lymphocyte telomere length by flow-FISH; chromosome fragility test to exclude Fanconi; gene sequencing.

Shwachman–Diamond syndrome

  • Autosomal recessive SBDS mutation (chromosome 7): ribosome biogenesis and spindle function.
  • Marrow failure (neutropenia most often) with AML risk; exocrine pancreatic insufficiency with malabsorption and failure to thrive; metaphyseal dysostosis, rib cage anomalies, short stature, skin rash.
  • No screening test: gene sequencing is needed. Differential: Pearson syndrome, cartilage–hair hypoplasia, cystic fibrosis.

Diamond–Blackfan anaemia

  • Congenital pure red cell aplasia of infancy, mostly autosomal dominant RPS19 (ribosomal protein) mutations; rarely GATA1.
  • Short stature, craniofacial, thumb and upper limb, heart and urogenital anomalies. May progress to aplasia, MDS or AML.
  • Diagnosis: macrocytic (sometimes normocytic) anaemia, reticulocytopenia, normocellular marrow with erythroid precursors under 5%, normal or low white cells, normal or high platelets; raised HbF and raised erythrocyte adenosine deaminase; gene tests.
  • Treatment: corticosteroids, transfusion, stem cell transplant.

Congenital thrombocytopenias

  • TAR syndrome: autosomal recessive thrombopoietin signalling defect. Severe bleeding in infancy, bilateral absent radii with thumbs present, heart and kidney anomalies. Platelets under 50 ×10⁹/L, leukaemoid white count up to 100 ×10⁹/L, few megakaryocytes, high TPO. Platelet transfusion; outlook good after infancy; no progression to aplasia or leukaemia.
  • CAMT: MPL (TPO receptor) mutations. Platelets under 10 ×10⁹/L, no megakaryocytes, high TPO; progresses to complete aplasia because MPL is needed by stem cells. Platelets and stem cell transplant.

11.5Aplastic anaemia

Acquired pancytopenia with a hypocellular marrow, no abnormal infiltrate and no increase in reticulin. Peaks at 10–25 and over 60 years, equal in both sexes. Grade it with the severity calculator.

Causes

  • Idiopathic in about two thirds.
  • Drugs: predictable, dose-dependent (cytotoxics, radiation, usually recover) or idiosyncratic (prolonged, unpredictable): chloramphenicol, sulphonamides, co-trimoxazole, chloroquine, NSAIDs (phenylbutazone, indometacin, diclofenac), gold, penicillamine, carbimazole, thiouracils, phenytoin, carbamazepine, some antidepressants, chlorpropamide, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, mebendazole, thiazides, allopurinol, ticlopidine.
  • Toxins: benzene, pesticides (organophosphates, lindane, DDT, paraquat), solvents and cutting oils; in rural areas, untreated water and contact with ducks and geese.
  • Viruses: seronegative hepatitis (severe, starts 2–3 months after the hepatitis, responds to immunosuppression), EBV, parvovirus B19, HIV.
  • Autoimmune disease (eosinophilic fasciitis, SLE, RA, Sjögren, coeliac, thymoma), transfusion-associated GVHD, pregnancy, PNH.

Pathogenesis

  • Fewer CD34+ stem cells with poor repopulation, more apoptosis and short telomeres; stroma and growth factors are normal.
  • Immune attack: activated CD8+ T cells release interferon-γ and TNF-α; fewer regulatory T cells; Fas up on stem cells; cytokine gene polymorphisms; response to immunosuppression; small GPI-deficient (PNH) clones escape the attack.
  • Genetic predisposition: HLA-DR2/DR15, telomere shortening.

Laboratory

  • Pancytopenia (one line early on), macrocytosis, reticulocytopenia, relative lymphocytosis; film with anisopoikilocytosis, toxic granulation and small platelets; high ESR; high NAP score.
  • Aspirate: easy, many fragments but few cells; few erythroid cells (often dyserythropoietic), megakaryocytes and granulocytes without dysplasia; prominent lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages and mast cells; early oedema and haemophagocytosis.
  • Trephine (essential): hypocellular, sometimes patchy (hot spots), normal reticulin, no abnormal cells.
  • Exclude inherited failure (anomalies; X-ray of radii), malignancy, hypoplastic MDS, megaloblastic anaemia, PNH, viruses, autoimmune and liver disease.
Diagnosis and severity
  • Diagnosis needs all of: two of Hb under 10 g/dL, platelets under 100 ×10⁹/L, neutrophils under 1.5 ×10⁹/L; a hypocellular marrow; no abnormal cells (macrocytosis allowed).
  • Severe: cellularity under 25% (or 25–50% with under 30% haemopoietic cells) and two of neutrophils under 0.5, platelets under 20, reticulocytes under 20 ×10⁹/L.
  • Very severe: severe plus neutrophils under 0.2 ×10⁹/L. Non-severe: the rest.

Treatment: protective isolation when neutrophils are very low, transfusion and infection care; immunosuppression with antithymocyte globulin and ciclosporin; cyclophosphamide; and haemopoietic stem cell transplant, the treatment of choice in young patients with a sibling donor.

11.6Acquired pure red cell aplasia

  • Children (transient): transient erythroblastopenia of childhood after a viral illness; parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis in chronic haemolytic anaemia.
  • Adults: idiopathic or primary autoimmune; secondary to SLE, RA, CLL and lymphoma, T-cell large granular lymphocytic leukaemia, thymoma (with myasthenia gravis), MDS, viruses (hepatitis, EBV, parvovirus B19), hypogammaglobulinaemia, drugs (azathioprine, isoniazid, phenytoin, anti-EPO antibodies with erythropoietin), major ABO-mismatched transplant and pregnancy.
  • Findings: anaemia, reticulocytopenia, and absent erythroid precursors (or absent late erythroblasts) in a marrow that is otherwise normal.
