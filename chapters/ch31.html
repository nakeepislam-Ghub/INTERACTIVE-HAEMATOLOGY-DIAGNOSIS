Coagulation factor disorders

Inherited deficiencies, from haemophilia to factor XIII, and the acquired coagulopathies: vitamin K deficiency, DIC, hyperfibrinolysis, factor inhibitors, liver and renal disease, APL, pregnancy, massive trauma and amyloidosis.

31.1Haemophilia

Inherited deficiency of factor VIII (haemophilia A), IX (B) or XI (C). Related terms: parahaemophilia (factor V), acquired haemophilia (anti-VIII antibody), autosomal haemophilia (type 2N VWD).

Haemophilia A

  • X-linked recessive. The intron 22 inversion causes about half of severe cases.
  • Low VIII → weak tenase → no thrombin burst → a friable clot that dislodges. Low thrombin also means low TAFI, so the clot lyses faster.
  • Neonates: cephalhaematoma, bleeding after circumcision. Infants: bruising. Walking children: joint bleeds.
  • Complications: target joints and arthropathy, nerve compression, intracranial bleeding, transfusion-transmitted infection, inhibitors.
Laboratory patternNormal platelets, bleeding time, PT and TT. Prolonged APTT that corrects with normal plasma. Low FVIII:C with normal VWF antigen and activity. Prenatal diagnosis by chorionic villus sampling at 11–14 weeks.

Haemophilic females: daughter of a carrier mother and affected father, Turner syndrome, extreme lyonisation, autosomal (2N VWD) or acquired haemophilia.

Treatment: recombinant or plasma-derived FVIII, desmopressin in mild disease, cryoprecipitate or FFP when nothing else is available, emicizumab and gene therapy.

Haemophilia B and C

B is less common and milder (deletions 40–50%, point mutations 30%). C (factor XI) is autosomal; bleeding follows surgery in tissues with high fibrinolysis (mouth, urinary tract) and does not match the factor level well.

31.3Vitamin K deficiency

Causes: poor diet, malabsorption, obstructive jaundice, liver disease, antibiotics, anticonvulsants, warfarin (blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase, so PIVKAs circulate).

Diagnosis: PT more than 4 times normal, plus normal platelets and fibrinogen with no FDPs, correction after parenteral vitamin K, or positive PIVKA.

31.4Disseminated intravascular coagulation

An acquired thrombo-haemorrhagic disorder: widespread intravascular fibrin formation that loses localisation, then bleeding as platelets and factors are consumed.

Pathogenesis
  1. Initiation: tissue factor exposure (sepsis, trauma, malignancy, obstetric catastrophes, snake bite, ABO-incompatible transfusion) and cytokines (IL-1, TNF, IL-6) drive excess thrombin.
  2. Failure of anticoagulants: antithrombin and protein C consumed; thrombomodulin and EPCR lost from injured endothelium.
  3. Fibrinolysis first rises (FDPs act as anticoagulants), then shuts down (high PAI-1 and TAFI).
  4. Results: microthrombi and organ failure, MAHA, bleeding.

Laboratory findings

  • Falling platelet count; schistocytes (not sensitive).
  • Prolonged PT, APTT and TT; a biphasic APTT waveform is an early sign.
  • Low V and VIII early; fibrinogen may be normal at first because it is an acute phase protein.
  • High D-dimer and FDPs; raised thrombin–antithrombin complexes, fibrinopeptide A; low antithrombin and protein C.

Treatment: treat the cause. FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets and PCC only for bleeding. Antifibrinolytics are contraindicated. Heparin is debated (consider in predominantly thrombotic DIC). Score it with the ISTH calculator.

31.6Acquired factor inhibitors

Autoantibodies (idiopathic in 50%; postpartum, autoimmune disease, malignancy, drugs) or alloantibodies after factor replacement. Acquired haemophilia A causes sudden extensive skin and soft tissue bleeding; haemarthrosis is uncommon.

  • Exclude lupus anticoagulant first.
  • Anti-VIII is time-dependent: the APTT corrects on immediate mixing but not after 2 hours at 37 °C.
  • Bethesda assay: 1 BU is the inhibitor that destroys 50% of FVIII in normal plasma in 2 hours. Residual activity 80–100% no inhibitor; 60–80% borderline; <60% inhibitor. Try the calculator.
  • <5 BU: high-dose FVIII. >5 BU: rFVIIa, aPCC or porcine FVIII, plus steroids, cyclophosphamide or rituximab; immune tolerance induction in congenital haemophilia.

The PT/INR overstates bleeding risk; thrombin generation is often normal. Viscoelastic tests (TEG, ROTEM) guide transfusion. In liver transplantation, the anhepatic and reperfusion phases bring hyperfibrinolysis.

Renal disease

Bleeding from uraemic platelet dysfunction and anaemia; thrombosis from endothelial damage, high fibrinogen, VII, PAI-1 and homocysteine. Nephrotic syndrome loses antithrombin in urine.

31.8Coagulopathy in acute promyelocytic leukaemia

About 80% of APL cases (especially the microgranular variant) have DIC at presentation, and bleeding causes around 10% of early deaths. The promyelocyte drives both bleeding and thrombosis.

In practice: start ATRA as soon as APL is suspected on the film, and support with platelets and cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate (Chapter 17).

31.10Coagulopathy of massive trauma and transfusion

Blunt trauma causes more tissue injury and more coagulation activation than penetrating (stab) wounds. Trauma swings from thrombosis to bleeding.

Why trauma clots

  • Tissue factor exposure and TF-rich microparticles from platelets, leucocytes, red cells and endothelium; brain injury releases brain-derived microparticles.
  • Endothelial damage exposes collagen and releases VWF, endothelin and thrombospondin-1.
  • Damage-associated molecular patterns: histones, cell-free DNA, HMGB-1 and mitochondrial DNA.
  • Inflammation: IL-1 and TNF raise monocyte tissue factor; IL-6 raises platelets; C4b binds protein S; thrombin cleaves C5 and activates PARs.
  • Loss of anticoagulants: less PGI2, NO, TFPI, tPA and antithrombin; shed soluble thrombomodulin; hypoperfusion raises TAFI.

Why trauma bleeds: the lethal combination

MechanismEffect

Thrombin itself has a very short half-life, so its generation is shown by soluble fibrin, fibrinopeptides A and B, TAT complexes, PF4, β-thromboglobulin and P-selectin, with falling antithrombin and protein C. In massive transfusion the platelets and fibrinogen fall, PT and APTT lengthen, but D-dimer stays near normal (use the explorer below).

Cardiopulmonary bypass adds haemodilution, platelet activation and dysfunction in the circuit, acquired VWD, high-dose heparin and hypothermia. The platelet count is lowest on days 1–3.

31.11Amyloidosis and micro­thrombo­embolism

Amyloidosis

Most often AL amyloid with liver and spleen involvement. Acquired factor X deficiency is the commonest defect (factor X binds to amyloid fibrils); VWF may also fall. Amyloid also coats platelets and vessel walls.

  • Clinical: "raccoon eyes" (periorbital purpura after coughing or a head-down endoscopy) and pinch purpura.
  • Laboratory: prolonged PT and APTT, low factor X, prolonged TT (dysfibrinogenaemia), platelet dysfunction, short clot lysis time, high FDPs.
  • Treatment: FFP, PCC, platelets, rFVIIa, desmopressin; treat the clone.
