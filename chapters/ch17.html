Acute myeloid leukaemia

17.1Definition and who gets AML

A heterogeneous, aggressive malignancy in which a haemopoietic stem cell or early progenitor is transformed. The clone of myeloid blasts expands in the marrow, blood or other tissues, differentiation is arrested, and normal haemopoiesis is suppressed.

  • Adults: the commonest acute leukaemia (80% of adult acute leukaemia); median age 65; slight male excess.
  • Children: only 10–15% of leukaemia. Neonates: the predominant form. APL incidence changes little with age.
Predisposing factors
  • Environmental: radiation, benzene, alkylating agents and topoisomerase II inhibitors, tobacco smoke.
  • Acquired clonal disease: CML, PMF, ET, PV, clonal cytopenias, PNH; also aplastic anaemia, eosinophilic fasciitis, HIV, thyroid and polyendocrine disorders.
  • Inherited: Down syndrome, Fanconi anaemia, dyskeratosis congenita, Shwachman–Diamond, Diamond–Blackfan, Kostmann and congenital agranulocytosis, congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia, familial platelet disorder (RUNX1), familial AML (CEBPA), Bloom, neurofibromatosis, Noonan, Werner, Rothmund–Thomson, Seckel, Wolf–Hirschhorn and others; a sibling with AML.

17.2Pathogenesis and clinical picture

Clonal evolution with cooperating mutations: a first mutation in a multipotent (or occasionally lineage-restricted) progenitor gives a small, stable clone. Further mutations create sub-clones that expand more aggressively, until acute leukaemia emerges.

17.3Laboratory diagnosis

Clonality is shown by an acquired cytogenetic change in all clonal cells, a clonal X-inactivation pattern (single G6PD isoenzyme or X-linked RFLP), or a clonal immunophenotype.

Blood count and film

  • Blasts: myeloblasts (large, 2–4 nucleoli, basophilic cytoplasm, Auer rods in about 15%); monoblasts (the largest, 3–5 nucleoli); megakaryoblasts (may look like small lymphocytes with cytoplasmic blebs).
  • Red cells: normocytic anaemia in 80%, mainly from reduced production; normoblasts; anisocytosis.
  • Platelets: low in 90%, under 50 × 10⁹/L in over half; giant and hypogranular forms.
  • White cells: WBC <5 × 10⁹/L in about half; ANC <1 × 10⁹/L in over half; dysplastic neutrophils. Pancytopenia in M3 and M7. Reticulocytes 0.5–2%.

Marrow

  • Aspirate: hypercellular, replaced by blasts (Auer rods in a sixth). 20% blasts is the threshold, but it is dropped for AML with defining genetic abnormalities (except BCR::ABL1 and CEBPA, which still need 20%). Dysplasia in 30–50%; sometimes eosinophilia or basophilia.
  • Trephine only for a dry tap, to separate a packed marrow from aplastic anaemia, and in follow-up. Mild to moderate reticulin (intense in megakaryoblastic leukaemia), increased vessels (VEGF, bFGF).

Immunophenotype

  • Immaturity: CD34, CD117, HLA-DR (HLA-DR negative in APL). Myeloid: CD13, CD33, MPO. Maturation: CD15, CD65.
  • Monocytic: CD11b, CD14, CD64, CD4, lysozyme. Erythroid: glycophorin A, CD71, CD36. Megakaryocytic: CD41, CD42, CD61.
  • Lymphoid markers (CD19, CD3) negative, though CD19 or CD7 can be aberrant.

Genetics

  • Abnormal karyotype in about 60%: +8, −7, −5/del(5q) (common after therapy), +21, −X/−Y.
  • Of normal-karyotype cases, 85% carry a mutation in NPM1, FLT3, CEBPA, KMT2A or NRAS. NPM1 or CEBPA without FLT3-ITD is favourable; FLT3-ITD is adverse.
Biochemistry, coagulation and other tests
  • LDH and uric acid raised (higher in monocytic types; urate surges after chemotherapy without allopurinol); hypercalcaemia (PTH-like protein), lactic acidosis, hypophosphataemia (uptake by blasts).
  • Potassium: hypokalaemia (tubular damage, lysozyme); pseudohyperkalaemia in hyperleucocytosis. Pseudohypoglycaemia and pseudohypoxia from blasts consuming glucose and oxygen in the tube. SIADH or diabetes insipidus; raised liver enzymes; renal infiltration; ectopic ACTH.
  • Coagulation: DIC in APL and monoblastic leukaemia; raised PF4 and thromboxane B2; low α2-antiplasmin, protein C and antithrombin (venous thrombosis).
  • Marrow culture: 85% form no colonies; return of colony-forming cells often precedes remission. Urine: haematuria and urate crystals.
  • Hypoplastic AML (2%): pancytopenia, no organomegaly, hypocellular marrow with blasts; separate from aplastic anaemia. Oligoblastic (smouldering) AML (10%): 3–20% blasts, striking dysplasia, high infection and bleeding risk.

17.4Differential diagnosis, treatment and response

  • Blasts mistaken for lymphocytes; marrow infiltration by lymphoma, neuroblastoma or carcinoma; leukaemoid reactions and non-leukaemic cytopenias (no leukaemic blasts). Immunophenotyping settles most.
  • APL versus pseudoleukaemia: recovery from drug-induced or Pseudomonas agranulocytosis gives a wave of promyelocytes, but with normal platelets, WBC often <1 × 10⁹/L, a prominent Golgi zone and no Auer rods.
  • M3 variant versus M5b; M4 versus M2, M5 and CMML; M0 versus ALL, M1, M5a, M7: settled by cytochemistry and flow.

Treatment: induction chemotherapy (combined or timed-sequential), then consolidation by chemotherapy or allogeneic stem cell transplantation according to fitness and risk. Supportive care: transfusion and fibrinogen, leukapheresis or exchange with hydration for hyperleucocytosis, intrathecal therapy for CNS disease, allopurinol for urate, phosphate binders. Myeloid sarcoma is treated as AML.

Measurable (minimal) residual disease in AML
  • Why: an independent predictor of relapse, disease-free and overall survival. The usual threshold is 0.1%.
  • When: at diagnosis (blood and marrow), after 2 cycles (blood) and at the end of treatment (marrow); then for 24 months, marrow every 3 months and blood every 4–6 weeks.
  • Flow cytometry: follow the leukaemia-associated immunophenotype (LAIP) found at diagnosis, or look for a "different from normal" phenotype when no diagnostic profile exists.
  • qPCR is the most sensitive and specific but applies to only about 40% of AML: PML::RARA, CBFB::MYH11, RUNX1::RUNX1T1, some KMT2A fusions, NPM1 (FLT3-ITD is less reliable). Blood is less sensitive than marrow.
  • NGS cannot use germline variants (ANKRD26, CEBPA, DDX41, ETV6, GATA2, RUNX1, TP53) or clonal haematopoiesis genes (DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1) as markers.
  • Newer methods reach 10⁻⁷: digital droplet PCR, single-cell and duplex sequencing, molecular barcodes.

What changed from WHO 2016
  • Genetic types are separated from types defined by differentiation; the term "AML, NOS" is gone.
  • The 20% blast requirement is removed for genetic types, except AML with BCR::ABL1 and AML with CEBPA mutation.
  • KMT2A, MECOM and NUP98 rearrangements are recognised broadly (often cryptic on karyotype); KMT2A covers more than 80 partners.
  • CEBPA now includes biallelic mutation or a single in-frame bZIP mutation.
  • "AML with myelodysplasia-related changes" became AML, myelodysplasia-related: morphology alone no longer qualifies; defining cytogenetics were updated and an 8-gene mutation list added.
  • Pure erythroid leukaemia is now acute erythroid leukaemia.

17.8Acute promyelocytic leukaemia with PML::RARA

FAB M3 and M3v; 5–8% of AML in middle-aged adults. t(15;17)(q24.1;q21.2) fuses PML (growth suppressor, pro-apoptotic) to RARA (retinoic acid receptor, needed for myeloid maturation). The gain-of-function fusion represses retinoic acid target genes and blocks differentiation.

  • Clinical: both forms cause DIC with increased fibrinolysis and early haemorrhagic deaths. The marrow clots rapidly and aspirates may be aparticulate.
  • Hypergranular APL: pancytopenia with few circulating abnormal promyelocytes; irregular, often bilobed or kidney-shaped nuclei; cytoplasm packed with large red-purple granules hiding the nuclear margin; large Auer rods and faggot cells (bundles of Auer rods).
  • Microgranular (M3v): very high WBC with short doubling time; bilobed nuclei and apparently agranular cytoplasm (granules below light-microscope size); can be confused with monocytic leukaemia, but a few granular or faggot cells are usually found.
  • Cytochemistry: MPO strongly positive in every leukaemic cell, covering the cytoplasm and nucleus.
  • Flow: high side scatter; CD117+, HLA-DR−, CD34− (CD34 and CD2 often positive in M3v, linked to FLT3-ITD); CD33 bright, CD13 heterogeneous; CD11a, CD11b, CD18, CD15 negative or weak; CD64+; CD56 in 10% (adverse). PML immunostaining shows a microgranular nuclear pattern instead of normal large speckles.
  • Genetics: PML::RARA by FISH or RT-PCR, with transcripts bcr1 (intron 6), bcr2 (exon 6), bcr3 (intron 3); cryptic or complex forms exist. Additional changes in 40% (+8, del(7q)); FLT3 mutations in 40% (ITD links to high WBC and microgranular form).
  • Variant RARA partners (ZBTB16, NUMA1, NPM1, STAT5B and others) give APL with a variant RARA translocation; ZBTB16::RARA and STAT5B::RARA resist ATRA.
  • Criteria: abnormal promyelocytes or microgranular blasts (may be <20%), PML::RARA, no prior cytotoxic therapy; t(15;17) is desirable.
  • Treatment and risk: ATRA plus arsenic trioxide, with anthracycline for high-risk disease; over 90% respond. High risk: WBC >10 × 10⁹/L. Low risk: WBC ≤10 and platelets >40; intermediate: WBC ≤10 and platelets ≤40.

17.9Core binding factor AML

Core binding factor (CBF) is a transcription factor: an α subunit from RUNX1 and a β subunit from CBFB. Both fusions disrupt it, need extra hits to cause leukaemia, have a good prognosis, and are diagnosed at any blast count.

17.11AML with NPM1 mutation and AML with CEBPA mutation

NPM1

  • The commonest genetic type: a third of adult AML, 6–8% of children; middle age, female excess, usually normal karyotype.
  • The mutation removes the nucleolar signal and adds a nuclear export signal, so NPM1 moves to the cytoplasm and drags tumour suppressors with it.
  • Usually myelomonocytic or monocytic; high WBC and platelets; cup-like (fish-mouth) nuclei in over 10% of blasts; multilineage dysplasia in 25% (does not change the diagnosis).
  • Flow: CD34 negative in most (CD34+ is adverse), HLA-DR often negative, CD117+; some look APL-like (CD34−, HLA-DR−, CD117+, MPO+). IHC: cytoplasmic NPM1.
  • Co-mutations: FLT3, DNMT3A, IDH1/2, RAS, cohesin. Prognosis good without FLT3-ITD; NPM1 + FLT3-ITD + DNMT3A is poor; rising NPM1 MRD predicts relapse.

CEBPA

  • 5–11% of adult AML. Biallelic CEBPA or a single in-frame bZIP mutation; some biallelic cases are germline (familial AML).
  • Needs ≥20% blasts. Usually M1 or M2; CD34, HLA-DR, CD13, CD33, CD15, often CD7. Favourable prognosis.

17.12AML, myelodysplasia-related, and other defined genetic alterations

AML-MR: 24–35% of AML, median age about 70, severe pancytopenia, usually multilineage dysplasia. Diagnosis needs all three:

  1. ≥20% blasts in blood or marrow;
  2. a history of MDS or MDS/MPN, or one defining cytogenetic or molecular abnormality;
  3. no prior cytotoxic therapy, no prior MPN, no AML-defining genetic abnormality, and no germline predisposition syndrome.

17.14Myeloid sarcoma

  • A tumour mass of myeloid blasts that effaces tissue architecture outside the marrow. Median age 46–59; in 2–9% of AML and 5–12% after allograft.
  • Usually with or as relapse of AML; may precede AML or mark transformation of MDS, MPN or MDS/MPN. Sites: skin, nodes, gut, bone, head and neck, gonads, CNS, lung. t(8;21) favours the orbit in children; inv(16) the abdomen; NPM1 and KMT2A give monocytic tumours.
  • Fish-flesh cut surface; green (chloroma) when MPO-rich.
  • IHC: CD33, CD34, CD68 (KP1), CD117, MPO; monoblastic forms CD68 (PGM1), CD163, CD14 and lack MPO and CD34; CD99 in over half; cytoplasmic NPM1 in 16%. Do not mistake for lymphoma.
  • Treated as AML; outlook as for AML, better for isolated skin or head and neck disease.
