42.1The iron profile

Iron tests fall into four groups: the serum iron profile (ferritin, iron and transferrin saturation, transferrin or TIBC, soluble transferrin receptor), hepcidin, red cell parameters, and tests of tissue iron overload. The clinical picture is in Chapter 2.

42.2Red cell iron markers

They show iron supply when the red cells were made, not today. They help diagnose iron deficiency early and separate it from chronic disease and thalassaemia trait, but any block in iron use (sideroblastic anaemia, lead, inflammation) affects them.

42.3Tissue iron

  • Liver biopsy: for liver damage with ferritin over 1000 µg/L and C282Y homozygosity, or unexplained high saturation and ferritin without it. Normal liver iron under 80 µmol/g dry weight (4.5 mg/g); hepatic iron index (µmol/g ÷ age) over 2.0 means overload. The best single measure of body iron, but invasive with sampling error. MRI is the alternative, though it cannot tell parenchymal from macrophage iron.
  • Marrow iron stain (Perls' Prussian blue): haemosiderin turns blue (ferric ferrocyanide); normal stores grade 1–3+. On Romanowsky stains, iron granules in red cells are Pappenheimer bodies.
StateSideroblastsMacrophage iron
Normal30–40% of normoblasts, one or a few small scattered granules; no siderocytes in blood (pitted by the spleen)Grade 1–3+
Iron deficiencyReducedAbsent or reduced
Anaemia of chronic diseaseReducedIncreased (2–3+, sometimes 4–5+)

Sideroblastic anaemiaRing sideroblasts: 6 or more granules around the nucleus or covering a third of it (iron in perinuclear mitochondria)Greatly increased (5–6+)
  • Marrow grading misleads when iron is unevenly split between macrophages and parenchyma (chronic disease); it is invasive and depends on the particles, the stain and the observer.
  • Chelation test: desferrioxamine 0.5 g IM, then 24-hour urine iron (normal about 2 mg/day).
  • Venesection test: weekly 450 mL venesections, allowing 3 mg absorbed iron a day, until iron deficiency; 450 mL of blood at Hb 13.5 g/dL holds about 200 mg iron.

42.4The Coombs (antiglobulin) test

The direct test (DAT) finds IgG or complement already on the patient's red cells; the indirect test (IAT) finds free antibodies in serum that can bind red cells. Clinical use is in Chapter 8.

Uses: autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, antenatal testing, haemolytic disease of the newborn, blood banking (crossmatch, antibody identification, phenotyping), haemolytic transfusion reactions.

42.5Red cell membrane tests

Osmotic fragility test

  • Indication: haemolysis with spherocytes and a negative Coombs test.
  • Principle: a biconcave cell can swell by about 70% before its membrane stretches; spherocytes have less surface for their volume, so they burst in milder hypotonic saline. Lysis at each concentration is read by colorimetry. After 24 hours at 37 °C cells run short of glucose, the cation pump fails, sodium enters faster than potassium leaves, and cells swell: fragility rises.
  • Sample: fresh heparinised or defibrinated blood (oxalate or citrate salts upset tonicity); test within 2 h at room temperature or 6 h at 4 °C; always run a normal control.
  • Method: saline 0.2–0.8% (plus 0% and 0.9% controls); 50 µL blood in each; 30 min at room temperature; spin 1200 g for 5 min; read at 540 nm, blank = 0.9% supernatant, 100% = water supernatant (absorbance about 0.7). For the incubated test use defibrinated blood for 24 h at 37 °C, add 0.7, 0.8 and 1.2% tubes, and use 1.2% as blank.
  • Normal: fresh, lysis starts at 0.45% and is complete at 0.35% (median 0.40–0.45%); incubated, starts at 0.7% and is complete at 0.2% (median 0.465–0.59%).
  • Controlled variables: blood to saline 1:100; pH (each 0.1 fall in pH acts like a 0.1 fall in concentration, so the saline is buffered); temperature (each 5 °C rise acts the same way).
  • Weaknesses: not specific (pregnancy, immune and other haemolysis, renal failure); misses mild spherocytosis.
IncreasedDecreased

Other membrane tests

  • EMA binding (flow cytometry): eosin-5-maleimide binds Lys430 of band 3. Reduced in hereditary spherocytosis, Southeast Asian ovalocytosis, CDA type II and cryohydrocytosis.
  • Glycerol lysis time and acidified glycerol lysis time: time to 50% lysis in buffered hypotonic saline with glycerol, which slows water entry.
  • Cryohaemolysis test: an ice-bath method that depends on the molecular membrane defect rather than surface-to-volume ratio.
  • Autohaemolysis test: 48 h at 37 °C with and without glucose. Normal 0.2–2% (0–0.9% with glucose). Spherocytosis: increased and corrected by glucose. Pyruvate kinase deficiency: not corrected.
  • Membrane protein electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

42.6Megaloblastic anaemia

Confirming megaloblastosis

  • Blood count: pancytopenia; high MCV and MCH, normal MCHC; large white cells and platelets.
  • Film: oval macrocytes, anisopoikilocytosis, fragments in severe cases, basophilic stippling, Howell–Jolly bodies, Cabot rings; hypersegmented neutrophils (early, gone 2 weeks after treatment); occasionally a leucoerythroblastic picture.
  • Reticulocytes lower than expected.
  • Marrow: hypercellular with a low M:E ratio; erythroid nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony (open lacy chromatin with normal haemoglobinisation), many mitoses and dying cells, multinuclearity, karyorrhexis, early forms predominate; giant metamyelocytes and bands (persist after treatment); large hyperpolyploid megakaryocytes; increased iron in macrophages and normoblasts.
  • Ineffective erythropoiesis: raised indirect bilirubin, urobilinogen and stercobilinogen, low haptoglobin, very high LDH (1000–10 000 U/L), raised serum iron, ferritin and lysozyme, high carbon monoxide output, sometimes intravascular haemolysis with high FDPs and a weakly positive Coombs test.
  • Chromosomes: random breaks, poor contraction, spread centromeres, exaggerated constrictions.

42.7Tests for PNH

  • Blood count: anaemia that may be macrocytic (reticulocytes), normocytic, or microcytic (iron lost in urine); pancytopenia when the marrow fails. Reticulocytosis; mild rise in indirect bilirubin.
  • Low iron, ferritin and saturation, high TIBC; negative Coombs test; low neutrophil alkaline phosphatase score.
  • Marrow (to exclude failure, fibrosis or infiltration): any cellularity, erythroid hyperplasia, reduced myeloid and megakaryocytic lines, dysplasia, absent iron.
  • Urine: haemoglobinuria and haemosiderinuria.
  • Ham (acidified serum) test: cells in fresh acidified serum (pH 6.5–7) at 37 °C; complement lyses PNH cells (type III 10–15 times, type II 3–5 times more sensitive than normal). False negative when type III cells are under 5% or type II under 20%, or after transfusion. False positive in HEMPAS (CDA II), whose cells lyse in acidified normal serum but not their own. Magnesium increases sensitivity.
  • Sucrose lysis test: iso-osmotic sucrose promotes complement binding; false positives in leukaemia and myelofibrosis.
  • Flow cytometry: red cells for CD55 and CD59 (both, to exclude isolated inherited deficiency); patterns are bimodal (50%), partial loss (10%) or trimodal with types III, II and I (40%). Neutrophils (CD16b, CD24, CD55, CD59, CD66b) are more sensitive, since PNH red cells die early and are diluted by transfusion.
  • FLAER: fluorescent inactive proaerolysin binds the GPI anchor itself; PNH cells do not bind it. The most sensitive and specific test, for white cells only.
  • Cytogenetics: trisomy 9 or loss of Y may be seen.

The disease itself is in Chapter 8.
