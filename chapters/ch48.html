Megakaryocytes and platelets

The stages of megakaryocyte maturation, how platelets are made, and platelet structure, granules and kinetics.

F5.1Overview

Megakaryocytes develop from the MEP (or directly from megakaryocyte-biased stem cells) under the control of thrombopoietin through its receptor MPL. Instead of dividing, the cell replicates its DNA repeatedly without cytokinesis (endomitosis), reaching 8N to 64N, most often 16N. The result is the largest cell in the marrow, 50–100 µm or more, with a single lobulated nucleus.

F5.2Stages

  • Megakaryoblast: hard to recognise on morphology; may show cytoplasmic blebs. Identified by CD41, CD42b and CD61.
  • Promegakaryocyte: larger, basophilic cytoplasm, nucleus beginning to lobulate.
  • Mature (granular) megakaryocyte: abundant pink granular cytoplasm, highly lobulated nucleus. Fragments its cytoplasm into proplatelets that extend into marrow sinusoids.

Each megakaryocyte releases about 1000 to 3000 platelets. Emperipolesis (other marrow cells passing through the megakaryocyte cytoplasm) is a normal finding. In a normal aspirate, megakaryocytes are not included in the differential count; instead they are assessed as present, reduced or increased, and their morphology is commented on. Small hypolobated or separated-lobe forms suggest dysplasia, which Module 3 covers.

F5.3Platelets

Platelets are anucleate discs about 1.5–3 µm across, surviving 7 to 10 days in the circulation. About one third of the platelet mass is pooled in the spleen, which is why splenomegaly lowers the count and splenectomy raises it.

F5.4Key points

  • TPO acts via MPL; megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis to 8N–64N, usually 16N
  • The largest marrow cell, 50–100 µm, with a single lobulated nucleus
  • Each megakaryocyte releases about 1000–3000 platelets
  • Platelets live 7–10 days; about one third are pooled in the spleen
  • α granules: fibrinogen, VWF, PF4. Dense granules: ADP, serotonin, calcium
  • GPIb defect = Bernard–Soulier; GPIIb/IIIa defect = Glanzmann
