HLH and histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms

Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis as a cytokine storm, its genetics and diagnostic criteria, then the WHO families of histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms: plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasms, Langerhans cell and other dendritic cell neoplasms, and histiocyte/macrophage neoplasms.

38.1Overview

Histiocytic and dendritic cell disorders fall into two groups:

  1. Inflammatory disorders, of which the key one is haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH).
  2. Neoplasms arising from, or differentiating towards, cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system: monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells.

38.2Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis

A life-threatening, uncontrolled proliferation of activated lymphocytes and macrophages that pour out inflammatory cytokines. It is one of the cytokine storm syndromes.

TypeCauses
Primary: familial HLH (autosomal recessive)FHL1 (locus at 9q, gene unknown); FHL2 PRF1 (perforin); FHL3 UNC13D (Munc13-4); FHL4 STX11 (syntaxin 11); FHL5 STXBP2 (syntaxin-binding protein 2, UNC18-2)

Secondary (acquired)Systemic infection, malignancy (especially lymphoproliferative), drugs such as phenytoin

Pathogenesis: loss of perforin or of the granule-release machinery means cytotoxic T and NK cells cannot kill infected cells and switch off the response. Activation continues, with excess interferon-γ, IL-6, IL-10 and M-CSF; macrophages become activated and eat blood cells.

Clinical picture: equal sex ratio; familial cases present around 1 year of age, and two-thirds occur in siblings. Usually triggered by infection in an immunocompromised host: viral (EBV, CMV), bacterial, fungal or protozoal. Fever, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, jaundice, rash and neurological signs (cranial nerve palsies, seizures).

Revised diagnostic criteria (Histiocyte Society): either a familial disease or known genetic defect, or 5 of 8: fever; splenomegaly; cytopenia in 2 or more lines (Hb under 9 g/dL, or under 12 in neonates; platelets under 100 ×10⁹/L; neutrophils under 1 ×10⁹/L); fasting triglycerides 3 mmol/L or more and/or fibrinogen under 1.5 g/L; ferritin 500 µg/L or more; soluble CD25 2400 U/mL or more; low or absent NK activity; haemophagocytosis in marrow, CSF or lymph node. Check a patient with the HLH criteria tool.

Other findings: reticulocytopenia; raised D-dimer; abnormal liver function; extremely high CRP, ESR and cytokines; raised sphingomyelinase; CSF pleocytosis and high protein; hypercellular marrow with histiocytosis and haemophagocytosis. Biopsy of node, spleen or liver is diagnostic: infiltration by lymphocytes and histiocytes with haemophagocytosis.

Differential diagnosis: fever of unknown origin, sepsis, multi-organ failure, hepatitis, autoimmune disease such as Kawasaki disease or SLE.

Treatment: corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, cytotoxic drugs (etoposide), intrathecal methotrexate for CNS disease, stem cell transplantation for familial or refractory disease, and treatment of the trigger.

38.4Plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasms

Mature plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferation associated with myeloid neoplasm

  • A clonal, low-grade proliferation of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) arising within a defined myeloid neoplasm: mostly CMML, also AML, MDS and MPN. Mainly men, median age 69–72.
  • Skin rash, macules and papules, rarely nodules. Driven by RAS pathway mutations (CMML) or RUNX1 mutations (AML).
  • Cells: medium-sized, eccentric round or oval nucleus with clumped chromatin, moderate amphophilic cytoplasm, sometimes vacuoles. Marrow: nodules rimmed by small lymphocytes (interstitial in AML).
  • Phenotype: mature pDCs at 2% or more of non-erythroid nucleated cells; CD123, TCF4, CD2AP, SPIB, CD303, CD304 and MX1 positive; aberrant loss of markers or gain of CD34, CD56 or TdT may occur. No specific genetic test.
  • Exclude reactive pDC increases: infection, SLE, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, Castleman disease.
  • Essential: mature plasmacytoid cells expressing CD123 and/or other pDC markers in a defined myeloid neoplasm. Desirable: aberrant pDC phenotype; absent or weak CD56.
  • Outcome follows the associated myeloid neoplasm, which it worsens.

Blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN)

  • Immature cells with pDC differentiation, with a strong tendency to involve the skin and then disseminate. Rare; any age, bimodal, but mostly the elderly.
  • Pathogenesis: non-activated pDCs with aberrant NF-κB signalling; ETV6 (12p13) deletion is early; complex karyotype; losses of CDKN2A, CDKN2B, CDKN1B, RB1, NR3C1; mutations in TET2, ASXL1, splicing genes (ZRSR2, SRSF2, U2AF1, SF3B1; inactivating ZRSR2 in men); NRAS, KRAS and ATM mutations are mutually exclusive.
  • Clinical: non-itchy bruise-like skin lesions on the upper trunk, head, neck and arms; cytopenias with fatigue, bleeding and infection; lymphadenopathy; sometimes a preceding MDS or MDS/MPN, or prior cytotoxic therapy.
  • Cells: medium-sized blasts, eccentric nucleus with fine chromatin and small nucleoli, grey-blue cytoplasm with vacuoles lined up under the membrane ("string of pearls"), sometimes pseudopods. Marrow infiltration 10–80%, residual cells may be dysplastic.
  • Cytochemistry: negative for MPO, non-specific esterase (α-naphthyl butyrate) and chloroacetate esterase.
  • Phenotype: CD123, TCF4, TCL1, CD303, CD304, CD4 and CD56 positive; CD3, CD14, CD19, CD34, lysozyme and MPO negative. Diagnostic: CD123 plus one other pDC marker plus CD4 and/or CD56; or any three pDC markers with none of the expected negative markers.
  • Differential: acute leukaemias with CD4, CD56 and CD123 (TCF4, CD303 and/or TCL1 with no strong lineage markers favours BPDCN); CD34 expression suggests AML with pDC differentiation instead.
  • Essential: blastoid cells with pDC differentiation by the criteria above. Desirable: no lymphoid or myeloid lineage markers, no CD34, high Ki-67.
  • Prognosis is poor.

38.5Langerhans cell and other dendritic cell neoplasms

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH)

  • A clonal neoplasm of marrow-derived myeloid dendritic cells with a Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a and CD207). Slight male excess; commoner in people of European and Hispanic descent; can occur with or before Erdheim–Chester disease.
  • Pathogenesis: MAPK pathway activation with phosphorylated ERK: BRAF V600E, and MAP2K1, ARAF, ERBB3, NRAS and KRAS mutations.
  • Single-system disease (older children and adults): a painful or painless lytic lesion of bone, or vertebra plana; may be multifocal.
  • Multisystem disease (neonates and infants): multiple bone lesions, soft tissue masses, skin rash, fever, hepatosplenomegaly with jaundice, endocrine failure with diabetes insipidus, and neurodegeneration (ataxia, dysarthria, dysmetria, learning and behaviour problems).
  • Laboratory: cytopenias, direct hyperbilirubinaemia, low albumin.
  • Cells: large oval histiocytes with a complex, grooved (coffee-bean) nucleus and moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm. Electron microscopy: Birbeck granules, tennis-racket shaped with a zipper-like core.
  • Immunohistochemistry: CD1a (surface), S100 (nuclear and cytoplasmic), CD207/langerin (Birbeck granules), cyclin D1, p-ERK, Golgi CD68; low CD45; Ki-67 usually under 10%. The mutation-specific VE1 antibody, with a sensitive DNA assay, detects BRAF V600E.
  • Distinguish from reactive Langerhans cells (dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, osteomyelitis, chondroblastoma) by morphology and phenotype.
  • Essential: large round to oval histiocytes with grooved to convoluted nuclei and a CD1a+ CD207+ phenotype in the right setting. Desirable: sensitive MAPK pathway mutation testing.
  • Prognosis depends on the stage at presentation.

Langerhans cell sarcoma

An aggressive neoplasm with a Langerhans phenotype (CD1a, S100, langerin; also vimentin, CD68, cyclin D1, HLA-DR) and high-grade cytology; MAPK mutations such as KRAS and BRAF V600E. Essential: pleomorphic histiocytes with high-grade cytology, many mitoses, and CD1a, S100 and CD207 staining (possibly focal). Desirable: rapid clinical progression; a MAPK pathway alteration.

Indeterminate dendritic cell tumour

A clonal histiocytic proliferation that expresses CD1a and S100 but lacks CD207/langerin. Essential: infiltration by Langerhans-like cells, S100+ CD1a+ CD207−, without the picture of multisystem LCH (lytic bone lesions, pituitary failure). Desirable: Ki-67 assessment.

Interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma

A malignant spindle to epithelioid cell tumour resembling interdigitating dendritic cells. Essential: spindle to epithelioid cells with abundant cytoplasm and indistinct borders; vesicular nuclei with or without grooves; S100+ (showing dendritic processes) with one or more haematolymphoid markers (CD45, CD4, CD43); negative for Langerhans cell, follicular dendritic cell and melanocytic markers.

ALK-positive histiocytosis in more detail

  • Origin: unknown, but the phenotype resembles juvenile xanthogranuloma, derived from a long-lived haemopoietic stem cell.
  • Genetics: ALK translocation, most often KIF5B exon 24 fused to ALK exon 20; rarer partners CLTC, TPM3, TFG, EML4, DCTN1, COL1A2, TRIM33. The fusion activates MAPK and other pathways.
  • Cells: variable mix of large oval (epithelioid), foamy and spindle cells; no pleomorphism, few mitoses. Epithelioid cells have folded, cleft or lobulated nuclei, fine chromatin, small nucleoli, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, sometimes haemosiderin and emperipolesis. Touton giant cells may be present. Spindle cells, common in breast tumours, form fascicles or a storiform pattern.
  • Immunohistochemistry: cytoplasmic ALK (rarely membranous or Golgi dot, never nuclear); CD68, CD163, CD14, CD4, lysozyme variably; sometimes S100, cyclin D1, OCT2; fascin and factor XIIIa often positive; CD30, CD1a and langerin negative; low Ki-67.
  • Prognosis is favourable. Infants with multisystem disease may regress spontaneously or remit with chemotherapy; death from progression is rare. Multisystem disease may resist chemotherapy and respond better to ALK inhibitors. Single-system disease can be cured by surgery.
