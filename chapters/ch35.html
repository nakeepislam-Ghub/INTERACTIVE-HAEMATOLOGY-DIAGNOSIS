Neutrophil, basophil and monocyte disorders

35.1Neutrophils

Neutrophils are granular white cells of innate immunity. They act by phagocytosis: granules fuse with the phagocytic vacuole, degranulate and digest its contents.

  • Output: about 1011 cells a day.
  • Lineage: haemopoietic stem cell → multipotent progenitor → lymphoid-primed multipotent progenitor → granulocyte–monocyte progenitor → myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band → segmented neutrophil.
  • Marrow retention: VLA-4 and CXCR4 on precursors bind VCAM-1 and CXCL12 on stromal cells. G-CSF breaks this bond and releases mature neutrophils.
  • Cytokines: G-CSF commits granulocyte–monocyte progenitors to the neutrophil line and drives marrow exit; GM-CSF, M-CSF and IL-3 also act.
  • Half-life in blood: 6–8 hours.

Pools: the marrow reserve (the largest), the marginating pool on vessel walls, and the circulating pool, which is the only one a blood count measures.

35.3Neutropenia

An absolute neutrophil count below the lower reference limit. Infection becomes likely below 0.5 ×10⁹/L and frequent below 0.2 ×10⁹/L.

Mechanisms: reduced production (hypoplasia or ineffective granulopoiesis), a shift from the circulating to the marginating pool, or increased destruction.

GroupCauses
InfectionViral: hepatitis, HIV, influenza, CMV, EBV, polio. Fulminant bacterial: typhoid, miliary TB, severe Gram-negative sepsis. Rickettsia. Parasites: malaria, kala-azar. Any severe infection in the elderly or debilitated.
Drugs (the commonest cause of isolated neutropenia)Antibiotics (chloramphenicol, sulphonamides, streptomycin, tetracycline); primaquine; isoniazid and PAS; phenytoin; dicoumarol; phenylbutazone; carbimazole; tolbutamide; thiazides; cytotoxic drugs

Peripheral pooling or destructionHypersplenism; immune (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis; Felty syndrome = rheumatoid arthritis + splenomegaly + neutropenia); anaphylactic shock
EndocrineMyxoedema, hypopituitarism
Chronic idiopathicNo cause found
Chronic benign neutropeniaOlder children and young adults (20–30 years), symptom-free, neutrophils 0.2–0.5 ×10⁹/L, monocytosis, hypocellular marrow, anti-neutrophil antibodies
CongenitalKostmann syndrome (severe congenital neutropenia): early, severe, recurrent infection with maturation arrest at the promyelocyte. Shwachman–Diamond syndrome. Reticular dysgenesis. X-linked hyper-IgM syndrome.
Cyclic neutropeniaSevere neutropenia (about 0.2 ×10⁹/L) every 21 days, lasting 3–6 days. Diagnose with a blood count three times a week for six weeks.

Clinical picture: poor resistance to infection, with little or no pus and a serosanguinous exudate holding few neutrophils; marked constitutional symptoms (high fever, chills, sweating, headache).

Laboratory: neutropenia without anaemia or thrombocytopenia (in the drug-induced type); toxic granulation and degenerative changes on the film; a very high ESR. The marrow shows absent granulocyte precursors with normal erythropoiesis and megakaryopoiesis, toxic granulation in the developing granulocytes, and may reveal the cause.

Treatment: prevent it by choosing drugs carefully and checking counts; stop the culprit drug; isolate the patient; give antibiotics guided by culture; give G-CSF or GM-CSF.

35.4Qualitative granulocyte anomalies

AnomalyFeatures
Pelger–Huët anomalyAutosomal dominant. Bilobed (spectacle) neutrophil nuclei. No clinical significance.
Pseudo-Pelger–HuëtAcquired: AML, MDS, infectious mononucleosis, malaria, bone metastases, drugs such as colchicine.
Hereditary hypersegmentationInherited; must not be mistaken for megaloblastic change.
May–Hegglin anomalyAutosomal dominant (MYH9). Thrombocytopenia with giant platelets (ineffective thrombopoiesis, bizarre hypergranular platelets) and Döhle-like bodies in granulocytes, with mild neutropenia. Serious bleeding is rare and infection risk is not raised.
Alder–Reilly anomalyCoarse, permanent azurophilic granules in all granulocytes and lymphocytes. Unlike toxic granulation, it does not come and go with infection.
Chédiak–Higashi syndromeGiant granules in neutrophils, pancytopenia, partial albinism and frequent infection. A trigger for secondary HLH (Chapter 38).
Myeloperoxidase deficiencyLow or absent MPO with some increase in infections. Neutrophils look MPO-negative on cytochemistry and some analysers.

35.5Basophils

BasophiliaBasopenia
  • Neoplastic: MPN (especially CML), AML with basophilia, carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma
  • Allergy or inflammation: drug and food sensitivity, rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis
  • Endocrine: diabetes mellitus, myxoedema
  • Infection: chickenpox, smallpox, influenza, TB
  • Hypersensitivity: urticaria, anaphylaxis, drug reactions
  • Cushing syndrome and corticosteroids
  • Hyperthyroidism

A persistent basophilia should always prompt a search for CML and other MPNs.

35.6Monocytes

MonocytosisMonocytopenia
  • Chronic infection: bacterial (TB, brucellosis, typhoid, subacute bacterial endocarditis, syphilis); protozoal (malaria, leishmaniasis, kala-azar); rickettsial; viral (CMV, EBV)
  • Neoplastic: AML with monocytic differentiation (M4, M5), CMML, CML and other MPNs, Hodgkin and other lymphomas, metastatic carcinoma
  • Inflammatory and autoimmune: SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, polyarteritis nodosa, ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease, sarcoidosis
  • Others: most causes of neutropenia (a relative monocytosis) and post-splenectomy
  • Aplastic anaemia
  • Hairy cell leukaemia (a classic clue)
  • CLL
  • HIV
  • Corticosteroids

A monocyte count persistently at or above 0.5 ×10⁹/L and 10% of white cells in an older adult raises CMML (Chapter 16).
