Anaemia of chronic disorders

Anaemia of inflammation and its iron block, and the anaemias of kidney, cancer, collagen, liver, thyroid and other systemic diseases.

Source: Dr Abeer's Red Cells Disorders notes, pp 113–121Reading time about 20 min
LectureIron studiesSystemic disease explorerCaseQuiz

9.1Definition and causes

Anaemia seen in chronic infection, chronic immune activation and malignancy, driven by a sustained rise in cytokines. It is the second commonest anaemia after iron deficiency.

  • Chronic inflammation: rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, sarcoidosis, scleroderma, inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Chronic infection: HIV, tuberculosis, osteomyelitis, chronic UTI, infective endocarditis, abscesses and bronchiectasis, fungal infection.
  • Neoplasms: lymphoma, myeloma, chronic leukaemias, carcinomas.
  • Organ failure: chronic liver and kidney disease, endocrine disorders.

9.2Pathogenesis

  1. Shorter red cell survival from cytokines (IL-6, IL-1, TNF-α, IFN-γ, TGF-β), fever and macrophage activation.
  2. Blunted erythropoiesis: less erythropoietin and resistance to it.
  3. Functional iron deficiency (the reticuloendothelial iron block): IL-6 raises hepcidin, which degrades ferroportin, so iron is trapped in macrophages, hepatocytes and gut cells; ferritin synthesis rises; transferrin falls; apolactoferrin steals iron back to macrophages.
  4. Add-ons: true iron, folate or B12 deficiency, bleeding, haemolysis, hypersplenism, drugs, marrow suppression and organ failure.

9.3Laboratory diagnosis

  • Mild to moderate anaemia in proportion to the disease: 70% normocytic normochromic, 30% microcytic; macrocytic in liver disease. Neutrophilia, monocytosis and thrombocytosis from inflammation.
  • Reticulocytes low for the anaemia.
  • Iron: ferritin high, serum iron low, TIBC low, saturation 15% or less.
  • Soluble transferrin receptor normal: sTfR ÷ log ferritin under 1 (over 2 suggests added iron deficiency).
  • Raised free erythrocyte protoporphyrin, hepcidin, copper, ESR and CRP.
  • Marrow: little erythroid hyperplasia; Perls stain shows iron in macrophages but few sideroblasts.

Practise separating it from iron deficiency in the iron studies interpreter.

Treatment: correct the cause; transfusion; erythropoiesis-stimulating agents; intravenous iron; anti-hepcidin drugs are being studied.

9.4Anaemia of chronic kidney disease

  • Low erythropoietin from loss of renal tissue (the main cause), plus inflammation.
  • Uraemic marrow suppression and shorter red cell life (chloramine in dialysis water); aluminium toxicity.
  • Secondary hyperparathyroidism and FGF-23 cause marrow fibrosis.
  • True iron deficiency from diet, malabsorption and dialysis blood loss.
  • Bleeding from uraemic platelet dysfunction, thrombocytopenia and dialysis losses; thrombosis in nephrotic syndrome (low antithrombin and protein C).

9.5Anaemia in other systemic diseases

Use the systemic disease explorer to compare causes across conditions.

  • Malignancy: chronic disorder, poor intake, GI bleeding (iron deficiency), folate use, AIHA, MAHA with mucin-secreting tumours.
  • Collagen disease: chronic disorder, drugs (GI bleeding, folate lack, aplasia), Felty syndrome, AIHA, renal failure.
  • Liver disease: macrocytosis, variceal bleeding, hypersplenism, acanthocytes, Zieve and Wilson haemolysis, marrow toxicity.
  • Pregnancy: physiological dilution (Hb ≤ 11 g/dL from week 8), iron and folate deficiency, MAHA and HELLP.
  • Endocrine: hypothyroidism (low EPO, macrocytosis, associated pernicious anaemia, menorrhagia), hypopituitarism, Addison disease, androgen deficiency, hyperparathyroidism.
  • Alcohol: macrocytosis, folate and B12 deficiency, iron deficiency, ring sideroblasts, hypersplenism, marrow toxicity, haemolysis.
  • Protein malnutrition and scurvy (with folate deficiency).
