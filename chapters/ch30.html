Platelet disorders

Too many platelets, too few, or platelets that do not work: thrombocytosis, inherited and acquired thrombocytopenia, ITP, thrombotic microangiopathies, platelet function defects and vascular purpura.

Familial thrombocytosis: dominant mutation of the thrombopoietin gene. Spurious thrombocytosis: counters mistake microcytes, red cell fragments, cryoglobulin, leukaemic cytoplasmic fragments or bacteria for platelets.

30.2Thrombocytopenia: causes and approach

Severity: mild 100–150, moderate 50–100, marked <50 × 10⁹/L.

Approach

  1. History: family history, travel (malaria), drugs and vaccines, transfusion, alcohol, quinine drinks, pregnancy.
  2. Examination: mucocutaneous bleeding, organomegaly, skeletal anomalies, limb ischaemia or skin necrosis (HIT).
  3. CBC and film: clumps (pseudothrombocytopenia), platelet size, blasts, dysplasia, hypersegmentation, Döhle-like bodies, schistocytes, spherocytes, parasites.
  4. Then: liver and renal tests, coagulation screen, D-dimer, LDH; marrow and immature platelet fraction (low in failure, high in destruction) when needed.

30.4Neonatal thrombocytopenia

  • Fetal or early onset (first 72 h): placental insufficiency, maternal ITP or SLE, neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia, congenital infection, inherited disorders.
  • Late onset (after 72 h): sepsis, necrotising enterocolitis.
Neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopeniaThe mother lacks a platelet antigen the fetus inherited from the father, most often HPA-1a (80%), then HPA-5b (15%). Maternal IgG crosses the placenta and destroys fetal platelets. The first pregnancy can be affected and it recurs in 80% of incompatible pregnancies. Intracranial haemorrhage is the danger. Treat with antigen-negative (or washed maternal) platelets and maternal IVIg.

Neonatal transfusion thresholds: transfuse below 30 × 10⁹/L; 30–49 only with extra risk (very preterm, bleeding, surgery); 50–99 only if bleeding.

30.5Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Acquired autoimmune isolated thrombocytopenia (<100 × 10⁹/L) with a normal marrow and no other cause. A diagnosis of exclusion.

Pathogenesis
  • IgG autoantibodies, usually against GPIIb/IIIa or GPIb-IX-V, opsonise platelets for splenic destruction and also inhibit megakaryocytes.
  • CD8 T-cell cytotoxicity, fewer regulatory T cells, Th1/Th2 imbalance.
  • H. pylori molecular mimicry; inappropriately normal thrombopoietin; hepatic clearance by the Ashwell–Morell receptor.
Childhood (acute)Adult (chronic)
Age and sexChildren, either sex20–45 years, mostly women
OnsetAcute, after a viral infectionInsidious, with other autoimmune disease
CourseSelf-limiting; 15% become chronicRelapsing and remitting

Phases: newly diagnosed (<3 months), persistent (3–12 months), chronic (>12 months).

Bleeding correlates poorly with the count because large young platelets work well: <20 purpura; <10 mucosal blood blisters; <5 risk of intracranial haemorrhage. No organomegaly.

Laboratory findings

  • Isolated thrombocytopenia with high MPV and PDW; giant platelets; no schistocytes.
  • Normal coagulation; DAT negative (excludes Evans); immunoglobulins, H. pylori, HIV, HCV, antiphospholipid, ANA and thyroid antibodies to exclude secondary ITP.
  • Marrow is not routine. Do it if over 60 years, with organomegaly, atypical features, relapse or before splenectomy. It shows normal or increased, small megakaryocytes with reduced budding.

Treatment aims for a safe count, not a normal one: corticosteroids, IVIg, anti-D, TPO receptor agonists, rituximab, splenectomy. Grade the result with the response calculator.

30.6Other immune thrombocytopenias

  • Post-transfusion purpura: an HPA-1a-negative woman sensitised by pregnancy receives HPA-1a-positive blood; severe thrombocytopenia 5–10 days later, self-limiting in 1–3 weeks. Treat with IVIg.
  • Drug-induced: hapten (penicillin), quinine type, fiban type (tirofiban, eptifibatide), abciximab, immune complex (heparin–PF4), autoantibody (gold). Definite diagnosis needs all four criteria: drug before the fall, recovery on stopping, no other cause, recurrence on re-exposure.
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia: IgG against PF4–heparin activates platelets. The count falls 5–10 days after starting heparin and the risk is thrombosis, not bleeding. Use the 4Ts score.

TTP is acquired (autoantibody against ADAMTS13) or congenital (Upshaw–Schulman). Ultra-large VWF multimers clump platelets. It is an emergency: plasma exchange, steroids, rituximab, caplacizumab. Platelet transfusion is avoided.

HUS: typical (90%, Shiga toxin E. coli O157:H7) or atypical (complement factor H defects, pneumococcal, drugs).

Acquired platelet dysfunction

Aspirin, clopidogrel, heparin; omega-3, garlic and onion; alcohol; uraemia; liver disease; cardiopulmonary bypass; DIC; MPN and MDS; paraproteins; vitamin C deficiency (vessel wall).

30.9Vascular purpura

Non-thrombocytopenic purpura: bleeding into the skin and mucosa from loss of vessel wall integrity, with a normal platelet count and normal clotting tests.

Fabry diseaseImmune: IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein), Behçet, SLE, polyarteritis nodosa
Hereditary haemangiomaDrugs (steroids, sulphonamides)
Congenital infectionMetabolic: scurvy, uraemia, hypercalcaemia
Psychogenic (autoerythrocyte sensitisation); dysproteinaemia

Congenital causes

  • Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu): autosomal dominant ENG (HHT1) or ACVRL1 (HHT2) mutations disturb TGF-β signalling in angiogenesis, giving telangiectasia and arteriovenous malformations. Family history, recurrent epistaxis, 0.5–3 mm mucocutaneous spots that blanch on pressure, visceral AVMs. Diagnosed clinically, with imaging and genetic testing; iron deficiency is common.
  • Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon: an infant's vascular tumour (kaposiform haemangioendothelioma) traps and activates platelets and consumes factors, giving thrombocytopenia, low fibrinogen, fragmented red cells and sometimes DIC.
  • Ehlers–Danlos syndrome: inherited defects of collagen or its partner proteins. Hypermobile joints, stretchy fragile skin, atrophic scars, easy bruising; arterial aneurysm and rupture in the vascular type.
  • Fabry disease: X-linked deficiency of α-galactosidase A; glycosphingolipid builds up in vessel walls (Chapter 39). Angiokeratomas, painful hands and feet, renal and cardiac disease. Enzyme assay and genetic testing.

Acquired causes

  • Purpura simplex: recurrent bruises on the legs of women, with normal tests.
  • Senile purpura: dark bruises on the extensor forearms and hands of elderly people as collagen support weakens.
  • Mechanical: raised venous pressure from coughing, vomiting, straining or tight bands.
  • Factitious: lesions in sites the patient can reach.
  • Infection: meningococcaemia, rickettsia, dengue; toxins or immune vasculitis.
  • IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura): after an infection, drug or food; IgA deposits activate complement. Palpable purpura on the buttocks and extensor legs, arthritis, abdominal pain with melaena or intussusception, and glomerulonephritis (haematuria, proteinuria, hypertension).
  • Scurvy: vitamin C is needed to hydroxylate proline in collagen; perifollicular haemorrhages, corkscrew hairs, bleeding gums.
  • Steroid purpura (collagen catabolism) and hypercalcaemia (calcified, rigid vessels).
  • Autoerythrocyte sensitisation (Gardner–Diamond syndrome): painful bruises in women, often after surgery or under stress.
  • Dysproteinaemia: hypergammaglobulinaemia, cryoglobulinaemia and amyloidosis (pinch purpura, raccoon eyes; Chapter 31).
Note on the source: Dr Abeer's notes list Fabry disease as autosomal dominant; it is X-linked, and heterozygous women can be affected.
