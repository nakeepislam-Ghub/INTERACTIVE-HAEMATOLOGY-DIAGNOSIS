Myelodysplastic neoplasms

Clonal stem cell disorders with cytopenias, dysplasia and ineffective haemopoiesis: causes, morphology lineage by lineage, the work-up, the WHO 2022 types and childhood MDS.

15.1Definition and the three thresholds

Clonal haematopoietic stem cell disorders with peripheral cytopenia, ineffective haemopoiesis, dysplasia of one or more myeloid lineages and a risk of transformation to AML.

ThresholdValue
Cytopenia (at least one lineage)Hb <13 g/dL (men), <12 g/dL (women); ANC <1.8 × 10⁹/L; platelets <150 × 10⁹/L
Significant dysplasia≥10% of cells in a lineage; megakaryocytes judged on at least 30
Blast count500-cell marrow differential; 200-leucocyte blood differential

The cytopenic lineage need not be the dysplastic one. Thrombocytosis is allowed in MDS with 5q deletion.

15.2Who gets MDS and why

  • Median age 77; rare under 40. Male predominance, except MDS with 5q deletion.
  • Primary (de novo): smoking, benzene, agricultural chemicals and solvents, family history, inherited marrow failure (Fanconi anaemia, dyskeratosis congenita, Shwachman–Diamond, Diamond–Blackfan), acquired aplastic anaemia.
  • Secondary: alkylating agents, radiation and chemotherapy (now classed as myeloid neoplasms post cytotoxic therapy, Chapter 19).
Four lines of evidence for the pathogenesis
  1. Multistep stem cell disorder: trilineage dysplasia; the same cytogenetic change in all dysplastic cells.
  2. Immune: hypoplastic MDS shares features with aplastic anaemia (macrocytosis, clonal T cells, PNH clones, response to anti-thymocyte globulin, evolution of AA to MDS).
  3. Apoptosis: cytopenias despite a hypercellular marrow; high TNF-α, FAS ligand and TRAIL; poor colony growth; low GM-CSF, G-CSF, IL-3 and IL-6 and a poor response to them.
  4. Angiogenesis: autocrine VEGF with over-expressed receptors.

Clinical picture: often found by chance; anaemia symptoms dominate (a third are transfusion dependent), infections and bleeding less often; organomegaly is unusual. Always exclude growth factors, drugs, infection, deficiency and immune causes of dysplasia.

15.3Blood and marrow findings

Blood

  • Red cells: anaemia in 85%, usually macrocytic; dimorphic and hypochromic with ring sideroblasts; ovalomacrocytes, teardrops, fragments, stippling, Howell–Jolly and Pappenheimer bodies, normoblasts. Reticulocytes low.
  • Neutrophils: neutropenia in 50%; hypogranulation, pseudo-Pelger–Huët (hyposegmentation), hypersegmentation, ring nuclei, pseudo-Chédiak–Higashi granules, Döhle bodies; paramyeloid cells (between monocyte and myelocyte).
  • Platelets: low in 25–50%; giant and hypogranular forms; normal or high in 5q deletion.
  • Blasts <20%, with or without Auer rods.

Marrow

  • Cellularity: hypercellular in most; normo- or hypocellular in about 10% (hypocellularity may be favourable). Trephine is essential; aspirates may be dry.
  • Topography: erythroid islands and megakaryocytes move to the paratrabecular zone; ALIP (abnormal localisation of immature precursors): clusters of 3–5 or aggregates of >5 blasts in the central marrow, a bad sign.
  • Dyserythropoiesis: megaloblastoid change, nuclear budding, internuclear bridging, karyorrhexis, multinuclearity, cytoplasmic vacuoles; ring sideroblasts on Perls; aberrant PAS positivity.
  • Dysgranulopoiesis (more prominent): hypo- or hypersegmentation, hypo- or agranularity, pseudo-Chédiak–Higashi granules, reduced MPO and Sudan black staining.
  • Dysmegakaryopoiesis: micromegakaryocytes and megakaryocytes with multiple widely separated round nuclei are the most reliable signs; non-lobated or bizarre nuclei, hypogranularity.
  • Fibrosis: grade 1 in half; grade 2–3 in 10–15%, linked to excess blasts and an aggressive course. Lymphoid aggregates and plasma cells may be present.

15.4Other investigations

  • Flow cytometry (supportive): low side scatter, weak normal antigens, CD15/CD16 asynchrony, aberrant CD7 or CD56 (CD7 is adverse), >2% CD34+ myeloid progenitors (adverse); erythroid CD71 or CD36 abnormalities. Aberrancies in ≥3 features and ≥2 compartments strongly suggest MDS or MDS/MPN.
  • Immunohistochemistry: CD34 (blast count in fibrotic or hypocellular marrow, ALIP), CD117 for CD34-negative blasts, CD61 or CD42b for micromegakaryocytes, p53 for TP53 mutation.
  • Cytogenetics and NGS: the backbone of classification and prognosis.
  • Other: poor colony growth; abnormal neutrophil and platelet function; raised HbF or HbH; i antigen up; low pyruvate kinase; occasional positive Ham test, autoantibodies or paraprotein.
Differential diagnosis of cytopenia with dysplasia

Megaloblastic anaemia, copper deficiency, aplastic anaemia, congenital dyserythropoietic anaemia, inherited Pelger–Huët anomaly (LBR gene), thalassaemias, sideroblastic anaemia, PNH, solid organ transplant, PMF, CML, AML, LGL leukaemia, drugs (G-CSF, chemotherapy, cotrimoxazole, tacrolimus, mycophenolate), heavy metals, alcohol, chronic infection, parvovirus B19 and HIV.

15.6The individual types

MDS with low blasts and 5q deletion2.5% of MDS, mainly women (median 67). Macrocytic anaemia, platelets normal or high (a third), PB blasts <2%. Marrow: many small, non-lobated or hypolobated megakaryocytes, erythroid hypoplasia, no fibrosis. del(5q)(q31–q33); SF3B1 in 20%; JAK2 or MPL occasionally. Good prognosis; lenalidomide gives transfusion independence in two thirds. Strong p53 staining in ≥1% of cells, TP53, RUNX1 and TET2 are adverse.
MDS with low blasts and SF3B1 mutation17% of MDS (age 70–75). SF3B1 splicing defect → mitochondrial iron accumulation → ring sideroblasts, ineffective erythropoiesis and iron overload. Dimorphic macro- or normocytic anaemia, no blasts; erythroid hyperplasia with dyserythropoiesis. The best outcome of all MDS. Exclude alcohol, lead, isoniazid, copper deficiency (zinc) and congenital sideroblastic anaemia (young, microcytic).
MDS with biallelic TP53 inactivationTwo TP53 hits (mutation plus mutation, deletion or cnLOH) → genomic instability and complex karyotype (>90%). More blasts, fibrosis and dysplasia; p53 overexpression on IHC. High risk of AML and death.
  • MDS-LB: no defining genetics, <5% BM and <2% PB blasts; anaemia in >70%; normal karyotype in most; mutations in TET2, SRSF2, ASXL1, DNMT3A, U2AF1, RUNX1. Exclude B12 and folate deficiency. Rare blasts in blood on two occasions may mean MDS-IB.
  • MDS-h: 10–15% of MDS, younger patients; cellularity <30% under 70 years or <20% over 70; CD8 T-cell attack (IFN-γ, TNF-α); LGL expansion; +8 and del(20q); responds to immunosuppression. Differ from aplastic anaemia (megakaryocytes and myeloid dysplasia) and PNH.
  • MDS-IB: 28–39% of MDS; ALIP, CD34+ CD117+ blasts with CD7 or CD56; −7, del(7q), complex karyotype; ASXL1, RUNX1, EZH2, RAS, TP53. AML develops in about 2 years with 5–9% blasts and within 1 year with 10–19%. Fibrosis and CD7 worsen the outlook.

15.7Childhood MDS

  • Childhood MDS with low blasts (refractory cytopenia of childhood): <18 years, <5% BM and <2% PB blasts; hypocellular in 80% and close to severe aplastic anaemia. Monosomy 7 is commonest (GATA2, SAMD9 or SAMD9L germline; SETBP1); adult-type splicing, TET2, DNMT3A and TP53 mutations are absent. −7, del(7q) or complex karyotype progress; −7 with SAMD9L in under-5s can resolve.
  • Childhood MDS with increased blasts: 5–19% BM or 2–19% PB; more cytogenetic changes and RAS pathway (PTPN11) mutations; exclude Down syndrome, JMML and AML with defining genetics. Transplant cures about 60%.
