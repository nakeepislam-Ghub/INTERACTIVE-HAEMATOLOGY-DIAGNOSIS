5.1Thalassaemia: definitions

Inherited disorders of the rate of globin chain synthesis, giving less haemoglobin per cell and an imbalance of chains that causes haemolysis. The commonest inherited haemolytic anaemia.

  • By chain: α, β, δβ, δ, γδβ, εγδβ.
  • By residual output: 0 (none) or + (some).
  • Clinically: major (transfusion-dependent), intermedia (anaemia and splenomegaly, not transfusion-dependent), minor (symptomless).

5.2β thalassaemia

Autosomal recessive, mostly point mutations: over 300 are known, but about 20 cause 80% of cases. They act at transcription (promoter), RNA processing (splice sites, cryptic splice sites, poly-A), or translation (nonsense, frameshift, initiation codon). Large deletions are uncommon.

  • Excess α chains precipitate in precursors: ineffective erythropoiesis and haemolysis.
  • Anaemia drives erythropoietin, marrow expansion (skeletal changes) and extramedullary haemopoiesis; splenomegaly causes hypersplenism.
  • Cells that make more γ chain (F cells) survive selectively, so HbF rises.
  • Severity eases with co-inherited α thalassaemia or high HbF; it worsens with extra α genes (triplicated or quadruplicated).

β thalassaemia major (Cooley anaemia)

  • β⁰/β⁰, β⁺/β⁺, β⁰/β⁺, or β thalassaemia with HbE or Hb Lepore.
  • Presents at 3–6 months (the switch from HbF to HbA): failure to thrive, severe anaemia, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Complications: thalassaemic facies, "hair on end" skull, fractures; hypermetabolism, gout, folate deficiency; gallstones; iron overload (cardiomyopathy is the commonest cause of death, plus cirrhosis and endocrine failure); bleeding from hypersplenism.
  • Laboratory: severe microcytic hypochromic anaemia; film with target cells, fragments, stippling and normoblasts; reticulocytes low for the anaemia; α-chain inclusions on methyl violet; high iron and very high soluble TfR.
  • Hb studies: HbF high (heterogeneous by acid elution), HbA low or absent, HbA₂ variable; globin chain synthesis α/β ratio 1.5–30; DNA analysis for confirmation and prenatal diagnosis.
  • Management: regular transfusion, folic acid, iron chelation, splenectomy when indicated.

β thalassaemia minor (trait)

  • Symptomless except under stress such as pregnancy.
  • Thalassaemic indices: little or no anaemia, low MCV and MCH, high RBC count, normal RDW (Mentzer under 13).
  • Normal iron studies and reticulocytes.
  • HbA₂ 3.6–7%, HbA over 90%, HbF 1–5%. If iron deficiency coexists, treat it and repeat: iron lack lowers HbA₂.
  • Not reliably diagnosed before 12 months of age without DNA testing.
Variant forms and interactions
  • Silent carrier: normal indices and HbA₂; found only when inherited with another β mutation.
  • Very high HbA₂ (~5%+): small deletions at the 5′ end of the β gene.
  • Normal HbA₂ β trait: co-inherited δ thalassaemia, α thalassaemia, iron or B12/folate deficiency.
  • Dominant β thalassaemia (inclusion body anaemia): exon 3 mutations; moderate anaemia, splenomegaly.
  • HbS/β thal: persistent splenomegaly and microcytosis. HbC/β thal: many target cells. HbE/β thal: major to intermedia picture.

Thalassaemia intermedia

A clinical label: not transfusion-dependent. It arises when a major genotype is softened (mild β⁺ alleles, co-inherited α thalassaemia, HPFH) or a minor genotype is worsened (extra α genes, HbE or HbC, δβ thalassaemia). HbA₂ is often higher than in trait with a high HbF.

5.3α thalassaemia

Common in sub-Saharan Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East and South-East Asia. Usually gene deletions (−α³·⁷, −α⁴·², or −−SEA and −−MED removing both genes on one chromosome); less often point mutations such as Hb Constant Spring.

5.4δβ thalassaemia, Hb Lepore and HPFH

  • Hb Lepore: a δβ fusion gene from unequal crossing over. Heterozygotes: thalassaemia minor picture with Hb Lepore 5–15%, HbF raised, HbA₂ normal.
  • Deletional δβ⁰ thalassaemia: heterozygotes have HbF 5–15% (heterocellular), normal HbA₂; homozygotes are intermedia with HbF near 100%.
  • γδβ thalassaemia: neonatal haemolysis; adults look like β trait with normal HbA₂.
  • HPFH: benign continued HbF production. Heterozygotes: normal indices, HbF 10–35% pancellular, normal HbA₂. It eases β thalassaemia and sickle disease when co-inherited.
  • Acquired thalassaemia: commonest is acquired HbH in MDS (especially with ring sideroblasts), myelofibrosis and erythroleukaemia; mostly men (ATRX).

5.5Sickle cell disease

HbS: GAG→GTG, glutamic acid to valine at β6. Deoxy-HbS is 40 times less soluble than deoxy-HbA and polymerises.

  • Triggers: infection, acidosis, dehydration, hypoxia (altitude, anaesthesia, exercise, cold).
  • Delay time before polymerisation shortens as HbS concentration rises, so dehydration worsens sickling.
  • Repeated sickling damages the membrane: K⁺ and water loss, phosphatidylserine exposure, adhesion to endothelium (VCAM-1, thrombospondin, VWF).
  • Haemolysis is mainly extravascular; free Hb consumes nitric oxide and promotes vaso-occlusion and a prothrombotic state.

Clinical picture

  • Silent in the newborn (HbF protects). Severity: SS > SC > SD > SO-Arab > AS.
  • Deaths: in the first 3 years from infection and splenic sequestration; in adults from stroke and lung disease.
  • Painful crises: hand–foot syndrome (dactylitis) in young children; back, chest, limb and abdominal pain later.
  • Acute anaemia: splenic sequestration (6–12 months), parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis, hyperhaemolysis.
  • Autosplenectomy and infection with encapsulated organisms; acute chest syndrome; stroke; gallstones; renal concentrating defect and papillary necrosis; priapism; proliferative retinopathy; avascular necrosis of the femoral head; leg ulcers; pregnancy complications.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Normocytic anaemia, reticulocytosis; leucocytosis and thrombocytosis from hyposplenism.
  • Film: sickle cells, target cells, polychromasia, and features of hyposplenism (Howell–Jolly bodies, normoblasts).
  • Sickling test (sodium metabisulphite wet film) or solubility test (sodium dithionate: turbid in HbS). False positives with leucocytosis, high protein or lipids; false negatives with low Hb, old reagent and in infants under 6 months.
  • Electrophoresis or HPLC confirms: HbS with no HbA in SS; HbF may be high. Newborn screening by HPLC, IEF or electrophoresis.
  • Low ESR and low osmotic fragility.

Management: penicillin prophylaxis and pneumococcal vaccine, folic acid, analgesia, fluids and oxygen in crises, transfusion of HbS-negative blood, hydroxycarbamide to raise HbF, stem cell transplant, gene therapy.

  • Sickle trait (AS): symptomless; risk with extreme exertion or hypoxia, haematuria and papillary necrosis; protects against malaria.
  • HbSC: milder crises but splenomegaly, sequestration and proliferative retinopathy; target cells and HbC crystals; high MCHC.
  • S/HPFH: benign, HbF 20–30%.

5.6HbC and HbE

  • HbC (β6 Glu→Lys): rhomboidal crystals, over 90% target cells in CC, mild anaemia and splenomegaly. On alkaline electrophoresis it runs with HbA₂, E and O-Arab, so confirm by acid agarose or HPLC.
  • HbE (β26 Glu→Lys, made at a reduced rate): mild microcytic anaemia with targets. HbE trait: HbE ~30%, HbA ~70%. Co-inherited with β thalassaemia it can be severe.

5.7High-affinity and unstable haemoglobins

  • High oxygen affinity: dominant point mutations at chain contacts or the 2,3-DPG pocket. Tissue hypoxia raises EPO and causes polycythaemia with no splenomegaly. P50 is low (curve shifted left); electrophoresis is abnormal in only half.
  • Unstable haemoglobins: over 100 variants that disturb the haem pocket. They precipitate as Heinz bodies, causing haemolysis worsened by oxidant drugs, with bite cells and dark urine. Show them with a heat (50 °C) or isopropanol stability test and methyl violet for Heinz bodies.

Try it: move pH, temperature and 2,3-DPG (2,3-BPG) and watch the curve and the P50 shift.
