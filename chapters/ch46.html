Granulocytes: neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils

Neutrophil maturation, granules, kinetics and function, with eosinophils and basophils.

F3.1Overview

Granulopoiesis moves from a mitotic pool (myeloblast, promyelocyte, myelocyte) to a maturation and storage pool (metamyelocyte, band, segmented neutrophil). The myelocyte is the last stage that divides. Transit from myeloblast to release takes about 10 to 14 days, and the marrow holds a reserve of neutrophils several times larger than the circulating pool.

A female "drumstick" (the inactive X chromosome) is seen on a small proportion of neutrophils in women.

F3.3Neutrophil granules

  • Primary (azurophilic): myeloperoxidase, neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, defensins, proteinase 3, lysozyme.
  • Secondary (specific): lactoferrin, lysozyme, collagenase, haptocorrin (vitamin B12 binding protein), cytochrome b558.
  • Tertiary: gelatinase (MMP-9).
  • Secretory vesicles: membrane receptors such as CD11b/CD18 that move to the surface on activation.

F3.4Neutrophil kinetics and function

Released neutrophils split roughly equally between a circulating pool and a marginated pool along vessel walls; adrenaline and exercise shift marginated cells into the count within minutes. Blood half-life is about 6 to 7 hours, after which they enter tissues and die there within a day or two.

  1. Rolling: selectins (L-selectin on the neutrophil, E- and P-selectin on endothelium).
  2. Firm adhesion: β2 integrins (CD11/CD18) bind ICAM-1. Absent in leucocyte adhesion deficiency type 1.
  3. Chemotaxis: towards C5a, IL-8 (CXCL8), leukotriene B4 and bacterial formyl peptides.
  4. Phagocytosis: enhanced by opsonins, IgG (via Fc receptors) and C3b.
  5. Killing: NADPH oxidase generates superoxide and hydrogen peroxide (the respiratory burst), and MPO turns hydrogen peroxide into hypochlorous acid. NADPH oxidase is defective in chronic granulomatous disease. Neutrophils can also release extracellular traps (NETs).

F3.5Eosinophils

12–17 µm, usually a bilobed nucleus, cytoplasm packed with large orange-red granules containing major basic protein, eosinophil cationic protein, eosinophil peroxidase and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin. Charcot–Leyden crystals are made of galectin-10. Production depends on IL-5. Counts are highest at night and fall after corticosteroids. They defend against helminths and drive allergic inflammation.

F3.6Basophils

10–14 µm, the least common blood leucocyte. Large dark purple-black granules often hide the nucleus. Granules contain histamine and heparin and are water-soluble, so they can wash out during staining and leave the cell looking empty. Basophils carry the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI) and express CD123 and CD203c on flow cytometry, but not CD117, which separates them from mast cells. A raised basophil count is an important clue to chronic myeloid leukaemia.

F3.7Key points

  • Myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band → segmented neutrophil
  • The myelocyte is the last dividing stage; secondary granules first appear here
  • Primary granules: MPO, elastase. Secondary granules: lactoferrin, haptocorrin
  • Neutrophil blood half-life about 6 to 7 hours; circulating and marginated pools are about equal
  • NADPH oxidase defect = chronic granulomatous disease; CD18 defect = LAD type 1
  • Eosinophils depend on IL-5; basophils are CD123+ CD203c+ and CD117−
