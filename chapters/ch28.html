Physiology of haemostasis

How blood stays fluid inside vessels and clots quickly when they are injured: the vessel wall, platelets, von Willebrand factor, the coagulation factors, the natural anticoagulants and fibrinolysis.

28.1The haemostatic system

Haemostasis is the process that stops bleeding, keeping blood inside a damaged vessel. It depends on a balance between two opposing forces.

28.2Blood vessels

Mechanical role of the endothelium

  • Smooth: prevents platelet adhesion and contact activation of coagulation factors.
  • Intact: prevents bleeding and exposure of blood to subendothelial collagen.
  • Negatively charged glycocalyx: repels platelets, which carry a negative charge too.
  • Small gaps: covered with platelets.

Chemical and biological roles

Vasodilators and inhibitors of platelet aggregation

Prostacyclin (PGI2): from endothelial cells, fibroblasts and smooth muscle; induced by thrombin, high blood pressure and trauma. Acts on G-protein coupled IP receptors.

adenylate cyclase ↑ → ATP → cAMP ↑ → Ca²⁺ release ↓ → vasodilatation + ↓ platelet aggregation

Half-life a few minutes; degraded to inactive 6-keto PGF.

Nitric oxide (endothelium-derived relaxing factor): continuous secretion by smooth muscle, macrophages, activated platelets and endothelium; inducible secretion from endothelium under IL-1, TNF and endotoxin. Raises cGMP → ↓ Ca²⁺ release → vasodilatation and ↓ aggregation. Half-life 3–5 seconds.

Ecto-ADPase (CD39) degrades ATP and ADP, inhibiting aggregation in response to thrombin.

Vasoconstrictors and stimulators of platelet aggregation

Thrombospondin-1: from platelet α granules and Weibel–Palade bodies; binds integrin-associated proteins → activates αIIbβ3 → platelet spreading and aggregation.

Endothelin: vasoconstriction and ↑ platelet aggregation; stimulates growth factor and cytokine secretion, neutrophil adhesion and macrophage chemotaxis.

Other endothelial products
  • Adhesion molecules (VCAM, E-selectin, P-selectin): leukocyte and platelet adhesion, inflammation, vascular permeability.
  • Anticoagulants: TFPI, antithrombin, heparinoids (heparan and dermatan sulphate), thrombomodulin, endothelial protein C receptor, protein S.
  • Fibrinolysis components: tPA and PAI-1.
  • Von Willebrand factor.

28.3Platelets

Megakaryopoiesis

HSC → common myeloid progenitor → MEP → megakaryocyte → endomitosis → proplatelets → platelets released into sinusoids
  • Each megakaryocyte gives about 2000–3000 platelets, which stay 2 days in the spleen before release.
  • Distribution: ⅓ in the spleen, ⅓ circulating, ⅓ covering gaps between endothelial cells.
  • Normal adult count 150–450 × 10⁹/L; life span 10 days; MPV about 7–11 fL.
  • Transcription factors: RUNX1, GATA1, FOG1. Inhibitors: TGF-β, PF4, interferons α and γ, IL-1, IL-4, LIF.

Structure

  • Peripheral zone: external coat with receptors; trilaminar membrane; submembranous microtubules (disc shape, contraction), actin–myosin microfilaments (pseudopodia, release) and the open canalicular system.
  • Sol–gel zone: mitochondria, Golgi, and granules.
  • No nucleus, but residual mRNA. Factor XI is membrane-bound; factor XIII is cytoplasmic.

Platelet receptors (the important ones)
  • ADP receptors: P2Y1 starts shape change; P2Y12 amplifies and stabilises (target of clopidogrel).
  • Thrombin receptors PAR1 and PAR4: ↓ cAMP, Ca²⁺ release, dense granule release, P-selectin exposure.
  • TP (thromboxane A2) receptors amplify activation; IP (prostacyclin) receptors keep platelets at rest.
  • GPIIb/IIIa (αIIbβ3): binds fibrinogen, VWF, fibronectin → aggregation and clot retraction.
  • GPIa/IIa (α2β1): collagen → adhesion. GPVI: collagen activation.
  • GPIb-IX-V: binds VWF and thrombin → tethering; responsible for ristocetin-induced agglutination in vitro.
  • Weak agonists: epinephrine (α2A), serotonin (5-HT2A), PAF.
G-protein signalling inside the platelet

Most agonist receptors (ADP, thrombin, TXA2, adrenaline, PGI2) are seven-transmembrane receptors coupled to a heterotrimeric G protein (α, β, γ). At rest the α subunit holds GDP. An agonist swaps GDP for GTP, and the α subunit and βγ dimer separate to signal until GTP is hydrolysed.

P2X1 is the exception among ADP-type receptors: it is an ATP-gated calcium channel, not a G-protein receptor. The thrombopoietin receptor (MPL, CD110) is few in number but very high in affinity; it clears TPO from plasma and primes platelets to other agonists.

Dr Abeer's notes attribute phospholipase Cβ activation to the Gi family in one line; it is the Gq family.

Platelet antigens
  • Shared with red cells: ABO (partly intrinsic, partly adsorbed from plasma), Lewis, Ii and P. ABO matters most in transfusion.
  • Shared with white cells: HLA class I (A, B, C). Antibodies after repeated transfusion or pregnancy cause platelet refractoriness.
  • Human platelet antigens (HPA): platelet-specific, mostly on GPIIb/IIIa. HPA-1a and HPA-5b are the most important. Antibodies cause neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia, post-transfusion purpura, refractoriness, and some autoimmune and drug-induced thrombocytopenias.

Platelet function in primary haemostasis

  1. Adhesion: VWF bridges GPIb-IX-V to collagen (tethering); GPVI, GPIIb/IIIa and GPIa/IIa are then activated.
  2. Spreading: filopodia and lamellipodia give the "fried egg" appearance.
  3. Primary release reaction: ADP from dense granules, TXA2 from membrane phospholipid, GPIIb/IIIa rises to about 120 000 copies per platelet, P-selectin is expressed.
  4. Aggregation: fibrinogen bridges GPIIb/IIIa on two platelets. ADP gives a loose, reversible primary plug; thrombin gives the irreversible secondary plug.
  5. Clot retraction: GPIIb/IIIa links actin to fibrin; myosin contracts and squeezes fluid out.
Key pointPlatelets are also procoagulant: phosphatidylserine on their surface assembles the tenase and prothrombinase complexes, and they carry FV, FVIII, fibrinogen, FXI and FXIII.

28.4Von Willebrand factor

A multimeric glycoprotein (500 to over 10 000 kDa), gene on chromosome 12, with two jobs: platelet adhesion to exposed collagen under high shear, and carriage and protection of factor VIII.

Cell-based model

  1. Initiation on tissue-factor-bearing cells: TF–VIIa activates X and IX. The small amount of Xa makes a little thrombin but is quickly blocked by TFPI and antithrombin. Not enough thrombin to clot fibrinogen.
  2. Amplification: this thrombin activates platelets, V, XI and XIII, and releases VIII from VWF.
  3. Propagation on activated platelets: IXa + VIIIa (tenase) activates X; Xa + Va (prothrombinase) produces a thrombin burst; fibrin forms and XIIIa stabilises it.

Non-specific anticoagulant mechanisms

Liver uptake of activated factors, pulmonary filtration, consumption, dilution by blood flow, and thrombin adsorption to fibrin ("antithrombin I").

28.7Fibrinolytic system

A tightly regulated enzyme system that removes unwanted intravascular fibrin, keeps ducts patent and clears thrombi.

  • Plasminogen (liver; stored in eosinophils) is activated only once it binds fibrin.
  • tPA (endothelium, chr 8): activity rises about 100-fold when bound to fibrin; half-life 2 minutes; inhibited by PAI-1.
  • Urokinase from renal tubules; streptokinase forms a 1:1 complex with plasminogen.
  • PAI-1 (chr 7): endothelium, platelets, fat; half-life 4 minutes; morning peak. PAI-2 from placenta, so detectable in pregnancy.
  • TAFI: activated by thrombin–thrombomodulin; removes C-terminal lysines from fibrin so plasminogen binds less.
Fibrinogen → X (D-E-D) → Y (D-E) → D + E  |  Cross-linked fibrin → D-dimer, D-D-E

Antifibrinolytic drugs: tranexamic acid, epsilon-aminocaproic acid, aprotinin.

28.8Haemostasis after trauma

  1. Vessels: reflex vasoconstriction and retraction; exposure of tissue factor and subendothelial collagen.
  2. Platelets: adhesion → spreading → release → aggregation → clot retraction.
  3. Coagulation: initiation → amplification → propagation.
  4. Anticoagulant forces limit the clot: antithrombin, protein C/S, TFPI, and fibrinolysis at the border of the injury.

28.9Haemostatic functions of the liver

  • Synthesis: all coagulation factors; protein C, protein S, antithrombin; plasminogen and TAFI; thrombopoietin; bile for vitamin K absorption.
  • Clearance: activated factors and complexes (thrombin–antithrombin, plasmin–antiplasmin).
