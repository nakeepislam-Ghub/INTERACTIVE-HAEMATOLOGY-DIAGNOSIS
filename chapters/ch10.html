The spleen and hypersplenism

Structure, circulation and blood pools of the spleen, what it does to red cells and to immunity, splenomegaly, hypersplenism, and life after splenectomy.

10.1Structure

Explore each region on the spleen map.

  • Capsule and trabeculae: a dense capsule whose connective tissue trabeculae support the organ and carry its vessels.
  • White pulp: lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic and plasma cells around arterioles in a reticular framework.
    • Periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS): the inner PALS is T-cell dependent, with interdigitating dendritic cells and migrating B cells; the outer PALS holds B and T cells, macrophages and, after antigen, plasma cells (mostly B cells, some follicular dendritic cells and CD4+ T cells, but no CD8+ T cells).
    • Follicles sit at branch points of the central arterioles; their B cells are IgM+/IgD+. The germinal centre is where B cells proliferate, mutate their antibody genes (somatic hypermutation) and class-switch; the mantle zone surrounds it.
  • Marginal zone: at the edge of the white pulp, blending into the red pulp. Marginal zone macrophages and non-circulating IgM+/IgD− B cells.
  • Red pulp (splenic cords): vascular parenchyma rich in sinuses.

10.2Circulation and blood pools

  • The splenic artery branches into central arterioles wrapped in white pulp; as they lose the sheath they open into the red pulp and its sinusoids.
  • Sinus endothelial cells have wide slits that act as a filter. Cells squeeze through, and damaged ones are eaten by the macrophages lying next to the sinuses.
  • Plasma skimming: plasma and leucocytes enter the white pulp; red cells stay in the axial stream and pass to the red pulp.
  • There are no afferent lymphatics; efferent lymphatics leave along the splenic vein.

10.3Functions

Filtration and quality control of red cells

  1. Conditioning: rigid cells with abnormal membranes or inclusions are slowed or destroyed, helped by active macrophages, tightly packed red cells, and low oxygen and glucose.
  2. Sequestration: reversible trapping by adhesion to the reticular meshwork.
  3. Culling and pitting: siderotic granules, Howell–Jolly bodies, nuclear remnants and Heinz bodies are removed while the cell is held.
  4. Phagocytosis: irreversible uptake of abnormal, aged or antibody-coated red cells.
  5. Reticulocyte maturation for 2–3 days in the cords: loss of inclusions, change in membrane lipids and a smaller size.

Immunity

  • Marginal zone: innate and adaptive immunity through macrophages and B cells. Clearing encapsulated organisms needs long contact with macrophages, which only the spleen provides.
  • White pulp: adaptive immunity through T cells in the PALS.

Extramedullary haemopoiesis

The fetal spleen makes blood, and can do so again with compensatory erythroid hyperplasia in severe anaemia, or in myelofibrosis and other diseases that take over the marrow.

  • Huge splenomegaly: CML, primary myelofibrosis, hairy cell leukaemia, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, lymphoma, Gaucher disease, and parasites (malaria, kala-azar, schistosomiasis).
  • Splenomegaly without the expected hypersplenism: Budd–Chiari syndrome, splenic vein embolism, heart failure, and infiltration (lymphoma, leukaemia, carcinoma, cysts, amyloid, lipid storage).
  • Symptoms: left upper quadrant or pleuritic-type pain when the stomach, bladder or bowel is full; back pain; early satiety; a palpable mass or splenic rub.

10.5Hypersplenism

  • Definition: overactivity of the spleen, causing cytopenias.
  • Primary (cause unclear): primary splenic hyperplasia, non-tropical idiopathic splenomegaly, primary splenic neutropenia or pancytopenia, splenic anaemia or thrombocytopenia. Secondary: any cause of splenomegaly.
  • Occult hypersplenism: the marrow compensates so counts are normal, until infection or drugs suppress the marrow and cytopenias appear.

Laboratory findings

  1. Cytopenias on the blood count; reticulocytes normal or slightly raised; sometimes spherocytes.
  2. Marrow normal or hypercellular, with normal early precursors and fewer late forms.
  3. Shortened red cell and platelet survival on isotope studies.
  4. Epinephrine mobilisation test (now obsolete): adrenaline releases marginated granulocytes, separating splenic sequestration from marrow suppression.
  5. Ultrasound or CT to size the spleen; splenic biopsy for the cause.

Treatment: total or partial splenectomy, splenic irradiation, liver transplantation (portal hypertension) and haemopoietic growth factors.

10.6Splenectomy and hyposplenism

Causes of hyposplenism

  • Splenectomy; congenital asplenia (rare); age over 65 (atherosclerosis and fibrosis); irradiation.
  • Drugs: methyldopa, intravenous immunoglobulin, corticosteroids.
  • Blood diseases: sickle cell disease (autosplenectomy), malaria, thrombocythaemia, myelofibrosis, lymphoma.
  • Splenic artery or vein thrombosis.
  • Immune: SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, hyperthyroidism, sarcoidosis, chronic graft-versus-host disease, combined immunodeficiency.
  • Gut: coeliac disease, Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis, tropical sprue, dermatitis herpetiformis.
  • Infiltration: lymphoma, Sézary syndrome, myeloma, amyloid, metastatic carcinoma (especially breast); nephrotic syndrome.

Complications

  • Surgical: bleeding (especially with thrombocytopenia), subphrenic abscess, infection after embolisation.
  • Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI): encapsulated organisms (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b, Neisseria meningitidis), also E. coli and Pseudomonas; septicaemia or meningitis that can kill within days.
    • Most frequent in the first years of life, so delay splenectomy until after age 5 when possible. Adults are protected in part by hyperplasia of other lymphoid tissue.
    • Why: slower clearance of encapsulated bacteria (no long transit), less specific IgM, weaker complement function and low IgA and IgE; IgG is unchanged.
Prevention of OPSI
  • Vaccines against pneumococcus, H. influenzae type b, meningococcus and influenza, 2–3 months before elective splenectomy (or as soon as possible after), with a pneumococcal booster after 5 years.
  • Antibiotics: oral penicillin (erythromycin if allergic), lifelong or at least for 2 years, and started at once with any infective symptoms.
  • Travel: antimalarial prophylaxis and meningococcal and Hib vaccines for tropical areas.
  • Patient education and an asplenia card.
