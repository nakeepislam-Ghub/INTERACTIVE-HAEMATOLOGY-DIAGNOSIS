Red cell enzyme disorders

G6PD deficiency and pyruvate kinase deficiency: how they cause haemolysis, how they present and how the laboratory proves them.

7.1Overview

Red cells have no nucleus or mitochondria, so they rely on glycolysis for ATP and the hexose monophosphate shunt for NADPH and reduced glutathione. Enzyme defects fall into four groups: glycolysis (ATP), redox, the shunt (response to oxidants) and nucleotide metabolism. The commonest are G6PD, then pyruvate kinase, then glucose phosphate isomerase and pyrimidine 5′-nucleotidase.

7.2G6PD deficiency

The commonest enzyme deficiency in the world. X-linked recessive, usually a point mutation giving an unstable enzyme.

  • Mechanism: oxidised Hb precipitates as Heinz bodies that stick to the membrane; the spleen bites them out (bite cells). Acute attacks are largely intravascular.
  • Triggers: primaquine and other antimalarials, sulphonamides and dapsone, nitrofurantoin, nalidixic acid, high-dose aspirin, naphthalene, methylene blue, vitamin K analogues; fava beans (divicine); infections; diabetic ketoacidosis.
  1. Acute haemolytic attacks (class II–III): well between episodes; mild to life-threatening haemolysis a day or two after a trigger.
  2. Favism: hours to days after (often raw) fava beans, mainly in boys: fever, abdominal pain, dark urine.
  3. Neonatal jaundice (days 2–4 onwards) that may need exchange transfusion and can cause kernicterus.
  4. Chronic non-spherocytic haemolytic anaemia (class I): exon 10 mutations; anaemia, jaundice, splenomegaly.

Diagnosis

  • During an attack: anaemia, bite cells, blister cells, polychromasia, Heinz bodies on supravital stain, marked reticulocytosis, low haptoglobin, haemoglobinaemia and haemoglobinuria.
  • Do not rely on a G6PD assay during the attack: reticulocytes have high G6PD and the oldest cells have already lysed, so the result may be falsely normal. Repeat 2–3 months later, or use DNA testing.
  • Tests: quantitative spectrophotometric assay; screening by the fluorescent spot test (NADPH fluoresces under UV), brilliant cresyl blue decolourisation or methaemoglobin reduction.
  • Management: avoid triggers, treat infection, transfuse in severe attacks; phototherapy or exchange in neonates.

7.3Pyruvate kinase deficiency

Rare, but the commonest glycolytic enzyme defect and the commonest cause of chronic non-spherocytic haemolytic anaemia. Autosomal recessive, usually an enzyme with low affinity for phosphoenolpyruvate.

  • Low ATP: the cation pump fails, cells become rigid and are destroyed in the spleen (extravascular).
  • High 2,3-DPG (upstream intermediates build up): the oxygen curve shifts right, so patients tolerate their anaemia well.
  • Clinical: from mild anaemia found in adult life to severe neonatal jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, gallstones and bone changes.
  • Film: polychromasia, sometimes shrunken echinocytes and spiculated cells, more after splenectomy.
  • Autohaemolysis markedly increased and corrected by ATP, not glucose.
  • Enzyme assay: about 25% of normal in homozygotes, 50–60% in carriers.
  • Osmotic fragility: low in fresh samples (reticulocytes) but high after incubation.
  • Treatment: folic acid; splenectomy guided by symptoms, not the Hb level.
